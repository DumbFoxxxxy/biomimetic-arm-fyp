T endon-Driven Robotic Shoulder Joint With
Passive Stabilization for Large Workspace
Sudong Lee , Graduate Student Member, IEEE , Kieran Gilday , and Josie Hughes
Abstract—The shoulder is important for humanoid
robots since it has a signiﬁcant impact on the arm’s range
of motion and its versatility. Therefore, research on the
shoulder joint, which is known as the spherical joint or
ball–socket joint, is ongoing to mimic its wide workspace
and high control accuracy. In this article, we propose a
shoulder joint with a unique tendon-routing method. This
method combines straight actuation tendons, passive heli-
cal tendons, and springs, preventing joint dislocation even
in extreme conditions and ensuring controllability in all
positions. We can also adjust dislocation characteristics
by changing the number of tendons and tendon-routing
method. The passive spring connections impose inherent
compliance and improve stability in harsh situations. The
tendons imitate human ligament and muscle in order to
achieve structural and functional similarity to a human
shoulder. The proposed joint has a ± 99.87◦ workspace
with respect to roll and pitch rotation and withstands high
impact with compliance. It has key advantages includ-
ing the wide range of motion, diverse dislocation map,
lightweight, and similarities with human shoulder motion
and structure.
Index Terms —Compliant joint/mechanism, mechanism
design, shoulder joint, tendon/wire mechanism.
I. I NTRODUCTION
T
HE shoulder joint is a highly mobile and versatile joint that
plays a crucial role in a variety of motions and tasks[1], [2],
[3]. Connecting the body and the arm, the shoulder is essential
in determining the performance of the arm, including range
of motion (RoM), payload of arm, and manipulation control
accuracy [4]. In human activities, the shoulder joint is heavily
involved in throwing, reaching overhead, and other dynamic
movements which enable many everyday tasks, for example:
reaching for objects, combing your hair, putting on clothes, or
lifting items overhead [5], [6]. The biomechanics of the human
shoulder joint, the glenohumeral joint, enables these capabilities.

have not been able to achieve performance similar to that of the
human shoulder [26], [28].
A. Related Work
V arious spherical joints, also known as ball–socket joints,
have shown a large RoM, high compliance, high control accu-
racy, whilst being lightweight[29]. One approach for wide RoM
with compactness is to make the ball in a ball–socket joint act
like a gear [25], [30]. A ball with special gear teeth intermeshes
with another ball or a gear connected with a motor. This approach
has reliable operation and high control accuracy due to the use
of a gear mechanism, and has also shows an improved RoM over
a conventional ball-socket joint. Abe et al. [25] minimized the
socket size to maximize RoM, however the workspace in the
pitch direction is still limited. Although Liang et al. [30] solved
this issue using permanent magnets, the absence of compliance
and a large number of parts remain a challenge. Other approaches
include more human inspired shoulders [26], [27], [28], [31],
[32]. These studies minimize the contact surface between a ball
and socket and actuate using tendons. Similarly, the shoulder

The muscles that actuate the glenohumeral joint are realized
through tendons connected to motors, and the ligaments that
prevent shoulder dislocation are represented by helical tendons
connected to springs. Although the exact geometry of the hu-
man shoulder is not perfectly replicated, we achieve structural
similarity by mimicking the functional roles of each component.
Building upon these similarities, it is possible to simulate various
scenarios of the human shoulder, such as dislocation, through
the variation of helical tendons that mimic the role of ligaments.
The use of helical tendon routing and the number of tendons can
both be adjusted to modify the joint’s stability and dislocation
characteristics. The speciﬁc design of the scapular and humerus
is now discussed.
1) Scapula: In order to enlarge the workspace of the joint,
we minimize the socket area in contact with the ball. Enclosing
the ball in the socket ensures stability and strength of the joint,
though mechanically restricts rotation at extreme angles. The
scapula has a concave socket with a depth of 5.5 mm for humerus
alignment. It contains ﬁxation points for springs and motors, as
well as anchoring holes for routing tendons.
2) Humerus: The humerus is designed based on a sphere
with a diameter of 40 mm. To increase the stability of this joint,
we propose a helical tendon routing. The helical tendon-routing
method keeps the humerus wrapped in tendons, improving RoM
and impact resilience. In this design, the humerus has helical
grooves and bolt ﬁxation points for tendon paths and anchoring,
respectively. In the case of a helical tendon, the pattern is carved
inward from the humerus’s surface, allowing the stabilization
tendons to be embedded within the ball. The shallow angle of
the helical pattern ensures that the yaw and roll · pitch rotation
tendons intersect at nearly vertical angles. Consequently, these
factors indicate that the pattern has a minimal inﬂuence on the
movement of other tendons. The pitch per groove is 18.66 mm,
and with three grooves, the apparent pitch is 6.22 mm. This pitch
provides enough turns for the tendon to wrap securely around
the humerus. In addition, the depth of each groove is 3.43 mm,
which prevents the tendon from coming out of the groove.
As illustrated in Fig. 2(b) and (c), three types of tendons
(stabilization, yaw rotation, and roll · pitch rotation) are an-
chored to the humerus. Three stabilization tendons pass through
the helical grooves before being connected via springs to the
scapular. These tendons, which provide compliance and stability
to the joint, allow the use of passive springs in the design to
reduce the number of required actuators and the control effort,
with minimal drawbacks. By applying a pretension in these
springs, the restoring force aligns the humerus when displaced
by other tendons or external forces, and also controls dislocation.
The restoring force direction is affected by the direction of the
tendon at the point where it separates from the humerus. This is
because the force exerted by the tendon when it wraps around the
ball can be considered an internal force within the system that
contains the tendons. The helical grooves increase the contact
area between the tendon and the humerus, and allow the tendon
to stably wrap around the humerus at any angle of rotation,
preventing the humerus from sliding out of the scapula. This
avoids dislocation at extreme rotation angles and helps to widen
the workspace.
In addition to the stabilizing tendons, there are a further ﬁve
tendons which enable control of the joint conﬁguration. Two

B. Model of Dislocation
We estimate the presence or absence of dislocation according
to tendon-routing methods or rotation angles. Dislocation is
deﬁned as when the center of the humerus is not aligned with
the center of the virtual circle created by the concave of the
scapula, which is equivalent to the humerus moving out from
the scapula. In other words, the direction of the net force by
tendons must be toward the inside of the socket (scapula) surface
where the ball contacts because there must be a reactionary force
from the scapula. The tendon forces connected to springs can
be estimated by calculating the change of spring displacement
with a geometric model. However, the tendon forces connected
to motors are not determined by geometrical constraints. To
estimate the dislocation with only geometric conditions, there
are two criteria. The ﬁrst condition entails conﬁrming that the

C. Fabrication
We fabricated the scapula and humerus as single-piece parts
by using a 3-D printer (Original Prusa i3 MK3, Prusa Research)
with polylactic acid ﬁlament. Particularly, complex shapes, such
as grooves, of the humerus are well-suited for production using
3-D printers. The scapula had room to stretch springs connected
to the stabilization tendons even at extreme position. The springs
installed with pretension do not exceed their elastic limit and
do not lose tensile force. The tendons (Zeus Line - φ0.6 mm,
Black-Cat) were anchored to bolts of the humerus, scapula, or
3-D printed pulleys for motors. The ﬁve motors (XL430-W250-
T, Robotis) were assembled by ﬁxing them to the underside of
the scapula. The tendon can withstand up to 59 kg and the stall
torque of this motor is 1.4 N ·m.

importance of the stabilization tendon is also veriﬁed in other sit-

deviates from the socket. When the three tendons are connected
to the humerus as we proposed [see Fig.11(a)–(ii)], the humerus
is not separated from the scapula and withstands free fall impact
(from about 30 cm height) of a 2.0 kg mass. The average peak
displacement of 11.65 mm and an average recovery time from
peak displacement of 313 ms. The displacement is limited, and
the recovery time is short relative to the timescale of motion
control. In the cases of the few or no stabilization tendons, the roll
· pitch and yaw tendons make the humerus return to its original
position after impact, but there is larger separation at the moment
of impact. The stabilization tendons prevent misalignment even
when high forces are applied. Furthermore, the springs at the
one end of the stabilization tendons absorb shocks and help the
humerus return to original position with restoring forces.
In the cases that the horizontal impact is applied to the
humerus [see Figs. 11-(iii) and S9-(iii) of the Supplementary
Material], the more stabilization tendons present, the less the
joint deviates from the socket. However, the deviations are less
than those of the vertical impact because of the concave shape of
the scapula. The concave shape also helps with alignment. When
we observe the ﬁnal states, the humerus returns the original
position with all four tendon conﬁgurations [see Figs. 11-(iv)
and S9-(iv) of the Supplementary Material]. In the cases of the
few or no stabilization tendons, the humerus comes close to
dislocation, but the elasticity of the roll · pitch and yaw tendons
allowed it to return to its original position. In summary, the
combination of tendon routing and transmission methods, the
use of springs, and the shape of the socket enables a spherical
joint that is impact resistant and highly compliant. Even when
subjected to external impacts during movement, the joint effec-
tively absorbs the shocks through compliance and follows the
predeﬁned path without any issues. (This can be see in Video
3 of the Supplementary Material.) Since the actuation tendons
and elastic stabilization tendons are arranged in parallel, high
compliance is not always guaranteed and exhibits directional
dependence. The detailed analysis is provided in Appendix J of
the Supplementary Material.
IV . DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
In this article, we proposed the shoulder joint with helical
tendon routing that enables three DoFs and a wide RoM without
dislocation, using simple structure and parts. Tendons as a
force transmission method does not degrade the compactness
of the joint. These advantages may extend the three DoF joints
to versatile usages without increasing the design and control
complexity. We developed and evaluated three different models
for controlling joint actuation. The wide RoM, control accuracy,
and compliance were veriﬁed in demonstrations. Especially,
tendon-driven force transmission allows high design ﬂexibility,
such as placing motors at a distance from the joint. For practical
applications, it is important to reduce the weight of components
located far from the center of mass (CoM) to get low inertia,
which enables agile motion and reduces the torque required for

control. Moreover, by enclosing the tendons in PTFE tubes,
the motors can be positioned with minimal interference with
the tendons from surrounding components or the complexity of
the tendon path. In other words, in order to enhance dynamic
performance, the motors can also be placed close to the CoM.
The ﬁrst contribution of this research lies in a tendon-routing
method. The helical routed tendons wrap around the humerus
stabilizing the ball, preventing dislocation and maintaining
controllable states even in extreme orientations. This minimizes
the socket’s enclosure of the ball for the wide RoM and allows
the joint to operate using a minimal number of actuators.
Since the helical tendon routing method utilizes only threads
and springs, it can be readily applied to other joints where
high stability is required, without signiﬁcantly increasing
weight, volume, or structural complexity. Moreover, it ensures
excellent compliance. This design feature can be widely applied
compactly to other joints by adding spiral grooves. Another
feature is control of the dislocation map in the workspace. We
are able to vary dislocation distribution by changing the routing
method depending on the number of stabilization, yaw, and
actuation tendons, as well as the anchoring locations.
Another contribution is durability and directional compliance.
The stabilization tendons connected with springs act as a shock
absorber. In addition, the tendon force transmission separating
the humerus from the motor prevents the motor from being
impacted when high external forces in a speciﬁc directional
range are applied to the humerus. The lightweight of the joint is
one advantage in terms of robot dynamics. All parts fabricated
by 3-D printing are lightweight, for example, the humerus is only
20.0 g. The tendon does not signiﬁcantly increase the weight of
the joint. This reduces the burden on the motor, making it easier
to control and improving agility.
The shoulder joint demonstrates promise, but there exists
room for improvement. The control performance can be im-
proved with more advanced approaches or improved hardware.
The control accuracy of a surface ﬁtting method can be increased
by using nonlinear ﬁtting method or adding nonlinear term to the
current polynomial equation (in Appendix E of the Supplemen-
tary Material). When using the machine learning method, the
results can be enhanced by changing networks or parameters.
Other machine learning approaches, such as long short-term
memory networks or reinforcement learning, can be applied to
further enhance the control. In addition, we can also improve
results with model-based learning by using the proposed control
model. Since control errors also come from friction and hystere-
sis, we can improve performance by lubrication or feedback
control. For example, embedding sensors, such as IMUs or
Hall-effect sensors on the scapula (with corresponding magnet
attached to the humerus), could be embedded to estimate joint
positions. These sensor signals would enable the implementation
of feedback control, improving control accuracy.
The maximum torque is low comparing the human shoulder.
Since the joint uses tendons as the force transmission mecha-
nism, the joint can be positioned remotely from the motor. This
allows for ﬂexible motor positioning, reducing constraints on the
use of larger motors. Because the joint torque is inﬂuenced by
the motor torque, changing the motor can increase the amount
of torque that can be generated. In this case, large force can be
transmitted without any issues by employing metal-based ten-
dons (e.g., Bowden cable) or multiple tendons twisted in parallel.
The shoulder joint can be used for various applications. Our
joint has the same number of DoFs and achieves functionality
through tendon-driven actuation similar to that of a human
shoulder. The tendons of the proposed joint correspond to the
ligaments and muscles respectively. Leveraging these functional
and structural similarities, a shoulder using this joint can act as
a test bed for wearables. In this case, the possibility to run a
number of experiments relatively quickly allows the developer
to modify design and to tune parameters in a fast feedback loop,
for example: the variable dislocation distribution may help to
explore shoulder support wearables.
