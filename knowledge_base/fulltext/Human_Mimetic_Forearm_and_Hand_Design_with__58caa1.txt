Humans have characteristic forearm and hand struc-
tures, and most of the previously developed hu-
manoids are not equipped with them. The human
forearm has a radioulnar structure composed of two
long thin bones, and the human hand has ﬂexibility
to move to ﬁt the object and strength to support the
human body. Therefore, we develop a novel minia-
ture bone-muscle module integrating bone and mus-
cle structures, and realize the human radioulnar struc-
ture. In addition, we develop a novel ﬁnger, which is
ﬂexible and robust, by using machined springs. We
integrate them and construct a forearm and hand sys-
tem which imitates human joint structures, muscle ar-
rangements, proportion, and weight. Using this fore-
arm and hand system, we realize several human skill-
ful motions.
Keywords: biomimetics, humanoid, tendon-driven
1. Introduction
In recent years, tendon-driven musculoskeletal hu-
manoids, which imitate not only the human proportion but
also every part of the human body, have been actively de-
veloped [1–4]. We have developed these humanoids by
imitating the joint structures, actuator structures, arrange-
ment of muscles, and weight of links, and have attempted
to realize human skillful motions. In previous studies,
screw home mechanism of the human knee [5], ﬂexible
spine [6, 7], shoulder structure composed of bladebone
and collarbone [8], etc. have been developed.
Of these characteristic structures, we will focus on sev-
eral points concerning the human forearm and hand, as
stated below.
• The human forearm has a radioulnar structure com-
posed of two long thin bones, which are not seen in
ordinary axis-driven humanoids.
• The human hand is so ﬂexible and robust that it can

support the weight of the body and endure large ex-
ternal force.
• The human hand is closely related to the forearm.
For example, the actuators to move the ﬁngers are
located in the forearm.
As for the reason we focused on these three points,
there are many studies about the radioulnar structure and
ﬂexible ﬁnger structure in the background. Regarding
the radioulnar structure, H. Endo et al. developed a fore-
arm with a radioulnar joint and tendons using corrugated
tubes, and controlled it using a human-like reﬂex con-
trol [9]. S. Ikemoto et al. developed an upper limb with
a radioulnar joint using pneumatic actuators [10]. Y .
Nakanishi et al. developed a radioulnar structure on Ken-
zoh [11]. However, actuators of the forearm developed by
H. Endo et al. are arranged not in the forearm but at the
center of the body, and therefore, the ratio of space occu-
pied by the actuators in the forearm is low and there are
no muscles of the wrist and ﬁngers. The upper limb devel-
oped by S. Ikemoto et al. uses pneumatic actuators, which
are easy to arrange but difﬁcult to control, and the forearm
structure developed by Y . Nakanishi et al. does not realize
multiple degrees of freedom (DOFs) because many actu-
ators cannot be placed in the f orearm. In addition, very
few previous studies discuss the merits of this radioulnar
structure or the arrangement of muscles. In this study, we
found the beneﬁts of the radioulnar structure from prelim-
inary experiments, which will be explained in Section 5.3.
There are several studies on robot hands that imitate hu-
man hands, such as [12–16]. Some of these can achieve
dexterous motion. In [12, 13], the developed hands have
many DOFs and are driven by many actuators. In [14], the
hand can grasp objects with appropriate ﬁnger arrange-
ments, thanks to the abduction and adduction movements
in each ﬁnger. In [15], ﬁngers have mechanical torque-
velocity converters, and in [16], ﬁngers have an underac-
tuated mechanism to achieve both dexterity and reduction
of mass and cost. However, they cannot support as large
a weight as human hands can. Concerning robot hands
that can support a large load, although a hand with reten-
tion mechanism [17] can lift an object weighing 50 kg,

2. Miniature Bone-Muscle Module
2.1. Overview
In this section, we will explain the realization of the ra-
dioulnar structure composed of two long thin bones. In
our laboratory, we have developed tendon-driven mus-
culoskeletal humanoids such as Kenzoh [11] and Ken-
shiro [24]. However, none of them have realized all hu-
man forearm and hand joints: the radioulnar joint, wrist
joint, and ﬁnger joints. The reason is the difﬁculty in
muscle arrangement. Conventional methods try to imitate
humans by attaching muscle modules, which include ac-
tuators and circuit boards, to bone structures. The muscle
modules of Anthrob [3] and those of Kengoro [25] are two
examples, and these humanoids are constructed by attach-
ing these muscle modules to the body structures. How-
ever, this method distorts the human proportion of the ulna
and radius, and therefore, detailedly imitating humans by
using many muscle modules becomes challenging. There-
fore, we developed a new miniature bone-muscle module.
We succeeded in making the muscle module compact by
using two strategies shown below.
• Integration of muscle and bone:
Each muscle module includes two actuators. This
creates space between the actuators, which we are
able to utilize. We integrate muscles and bones by
placing bone structure parts in this space, and this en-
ables the construction of a compact muscle module
without wastefully separating muscle modules and
bones. In addition, by using common parts, there is
a large beneﬁt of reducing the volume.
• Adoption of miniature motors, and heat dissipation
by the contact of motors and bone structures:
Using miniature motors with high reduction gear is
the easiest method in order to make the muscle mod-
ule compact. However, using a high reduction gear is
not a good idea for getting a large torque in terms of
backdrivability. In addition, miniature motors heat

2.2. Development of Miniature Bone-Muscle
Module
The details of the newly developed miniature bone-
muscle module are shown in Fig. 2. Brushless DC motors
are used in this module, and their gear ratios are 84 : 1
or 157 : 1, depending on the muscle. The muscle wire is
Dyneema, and it is wound by a pulley. The loadcells that
measure muscle tension, thermo sensors attached to mo-
tors, and hall sensors in motors, are all connected to motor
drivers, and circuit covers are placed to protect them in or-
der to keep the system stable.
This muscle module has three noteworthy points. First,
this module can be used as the bone structure, because the
“base of bone” and “support of bone” in Fig. 2 become
the bone structure. In other words, the bone structure can
be composed without additional body parts by connecting
these muscle modules lengthwise and crosswise. Second,
a heat transfer sheet is placed between “base of bone” and
“motor,” through which heat generated in the motors is
dissipated to the bone structure. This enables the motors
to keep continuous high muscle tension. Third, we devel-
oped a new ultra-tiny tension measurement unit as shown
in Fig. 3 . Its volume is 61% of the previous unit’s vol-
ume [25], because the limited space is used effectively
by placing a loadcell, which determines the unit size, in

this unit can measure muscle tension up to 56.5 kgf. In

Humans can support themselves using their hands, such as
when dangling from a bar, putting weight on the ground or
walls, and stabilizing the body by holding a handrail, and
can act in various environments, when compared to using
only their feet. Thus, we attempted to develop hands that
can support the body weight. For this purpose, the hands
need not only to be able to bring out a large force but also
not to be broken by a large load. Thus, we used ﬂexi-
ble machined springs in each ﬁnger joint, and developed
a robust and ﬂexible hand which can stand large loads and
external impacts.
3.2. Fingers Using Machined Springs
The axis joint [12], rubber joint [28], and spring
joint [29] are some of the frequently used structures of
a robot ﬁnger joint. Using these as a ﬁnger joint, which
is thin and small, is not adequate because the axis joint
is weak against an external impact, the rubber joint does
not have enough strength, and connecting an ordinary coil
spring joint to other parts is difﬁcult. Therefore, we use
machined springs, as shown in Fig. 7, as the ﬁnger joints.
First, the beneﬁts of using springs as a ﬁnger joint are
stated below.
• Springs have high strength and ﬂexibility, and there-
fore, can absorb external impacts.
• Actuators to extend ﬁngers are not needed; therefore,
actuators to ﬂex ﬁngers can use more space and their

• A restoring force weakens the torque exerted by ac-

The ﬁrst disadvantage is canceled by the advantage men-

space and weight instead of those to extend ﬁngers. In
order to cancel the second disadvantage, we place some
plates near the spring joints, the detail of which will be
described later. Based on these characteristics of the ma-
chined spring, we can develop a ﬁnger structure which
is more shock-resistant than the axis joint, can support a
larger load than the rubber joint, and has a ﬁrmer structure
than the ordinary coil spring joint.

4.2. Human Mimetic Hand Design
We show the newly developed hand using the machined
springs stated in Section 3, in Fig. 12 . The carpals con-
nect to the wrist joint, which is composed of a universal
joint, and ﬁve ﬁngers connect to the carpals. In order to
make grasping possible with the limited number of mus-
cles equipped in the hand, we arranged the thumb in the
opposite direction of the other four ﬁngers. When plac-
ing the hands on a wall, the thumb, equipped with ﬂexible
joints composed of machined springs, bends by the reac-
tion force from the wall, and the plane of the palms can be
pressed to the wall. The palm is composed of a polycar-

bones of the four ﬁngers. In addition, loadcells are ar-
ranged under this plate as well as in ﬁngertips ( Fig. 13 ),
and this hand can measure the reaction force of the palm.
Loadcells in the hand and an ampliﬁer circuit near the
wrist are connected by shield cables, and we have not ob-
served the inﬂuence of noise.

4.3. Forearm and Hand Actuation System
The actuation system and each role of the muscles are
shown in Fig. 14. Since the radius and ulna are each com-
posed of two miniature bone-muscle modules, the total
number of muscles is eight, and two of them move only
the radioulnar joint. In addition, two muscles move only
the wrist joint, and one is a polyarticular muscle which
moves the wrist and radioulnar joint simultaneously. The
remaining three muscles can actuate ﬁngers and simulta-
neously move the wrist joint. Three muscles actuate the
thumb, index and middle ﬁngers, and ring and little ﬁn-
gers, respectively. Regarding the latter two muscles, the
respective groups of two ﬁngers are actuated by one wire,
and the branch point of this wire is composed of a mov-
able pulley [30] for bending ﬁngers along the surface of
objects.
The important point here is the relationship between
the movements of ﬁngers and radioulnar/wrist joints. In
humans, since the muscles actuating ﬁngers are arranged
along the long thin radius and ulna, the ﬁnger muscles
are hardly affected by the rotation of the radioulnar joint.
However, we cannot design the thin bones completely as
in humans due to the size of muscle modules, and arrang-
ing muscles along the bones as in humans is not realis-
tic due to friction. Therefore, as shown in Fig. 15 ,w e

4.4. Forearm and Hand Circuit System
The circuit conﬁguration of the forearm and hand
is shown in Fig. 16 . As this forearm-hand system is
equipped to the tendon-driven musculoskeletal humanoid
Kengoro [4] and the circuit communication of Kengoro
is USB communication, we need to conﬁgure suitable
circuits for it. Motor drivers are included in each mus-
cle module, and control boards of motor drivers (control
board), USB hubs, and loadcell ampliﬁcation boards for
hand force sensor (loadcell board) are equipped to the side
of the radius. Control boards and a loadcell board connect
to a USB hub, and each control board connects to three

5.3. Beneﬁts of the Radioulnar Structure

the radius, the distal of the ulna, and near the little
ﬁnger.
3. Twisting of the skin can be dispersed by its two long
bones.

and so on. These motions are made possible by three

and twisting hardly occurs. We show the measured yaw

6. Discussion
We will discuss the advantages and disadvantages of
this study while comparing the developed forearm of Ken-
goro and the forearm of the latest musculoskeletal hu-
manoid Musashi [23]. The design concept of Musashi
is a simple and modularized structure which can be eas-
ily constructed and reconstructed for the investigation of
learning control systems. We show the overview of its
forearm and hand in Fig. 26. Four miniature bone-muscle
modules are parallelly arranged in the forearm. The fore-
arm has no radioulnar joint. The yaw joint is designed as
the elbow yaw joint, and therefore, all eight muscles can
be used for only wrist and ﬁnger joints.
We show the comparison between Kengoro and
Musashi, regarding the beneﬁts of the radioulnar struc-
ture, maintainability, human-like forearm length, and

or muscle modules that are not ﬁxed to the bone struc-
ture [36] like pneumatic actuators. However, the latter de-
creases reliability and maintainability. The simultaneous
realization of the radioulnar joint and its maintainability
will be explored in our future work.
7. Conclusions
In this study, we explained the design method of human
mimetic forearm and hand, and its applications. First,
the human forearm is composed of two long thin bones,
and to realize this, we explained the design of a miniature
bone-muscle module integrating bone and muscle, and a
compact design of the forearm using these modules. In
addition, we were able to realize a continuous high mus-
cle tension by heat dissipation from muscle to bone struc-
tures. Second, in order to realize a ﬂexible and robust
hand, we developed a ﬁnger joint using machined springs.
By using these joints, we could construct a hand that is
not broken when hit with a hammer and can grasp vari-
ous objects. Finally, by integrating these design methods,
we succeeded in constructing a human mimetic forearm
and hand without deviating from the human proportion,
weight, muscle arrangement , and bone struct ure. Utiliz-
ing the beneﬁts of this forearm and hand system, we con-
ducted several experiments such as opening a book, dan-
gling, and badminton swinging, and validated their effec-
tiveness.
In future works, we would like to study the control and
learning method of more skillful human motions using
this forearm and hand system. In addition, we would like
to construct a more ﬂexible and multi-DOF body that im-
itates human beings in more detail.
