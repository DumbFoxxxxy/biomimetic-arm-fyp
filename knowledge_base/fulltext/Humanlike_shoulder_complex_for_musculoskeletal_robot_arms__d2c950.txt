Humanlike Shoulder Complex for Musculoskeletal Robot Arms
Shuhei Ikemoto 1, Fumiya Kannou 2 and Koh Hosoda 1
Abstract— In recent years, musculoskeletal robots are being
intensively studied to exploit the advantages of biological mus-
culoskeletal systems for robot d evelopments. In these robots,
it is very important to assure engineering, biomechanical,
and anatomical plausibility at the same time. However , these
requirements are often in contradiction. Especially, in the
human’s shoulder complex, mimicking the glenohumeral joint
and the scapulothoracic joint has been difﬁcult because of
the need to assure a wide range of movement and the joint’s
stability at the same time. In this paper , we propose mechan-
ical structures to realize the functions of the glenohumeral
joints and scapulothoracic joint. These structures were used
to develop a musculoskeletal robot arm driven by pneumatic
artiﬁcial muscles. In addition, i n order to verify the feasibility
of the robot arm, we present a dynamic motion in which the
robot arm throws a ball by a simple control strategy.
I. I NTRODUCTION
Musculoskeletal systems in living organisms improved
during their evolutionary history. Therefore, these structures
must reﬂect the needs for surviv ing in their environments.
The approach of mimicking the musculoskeletal system to
exploit its advantages has attr acted considerable attention
in robotics. Furthermore, it is also expected that ﬁndings
obtained by developing these robots will contribute to other
research ﬁelds such as biomechanics.
The superior limb girdle is fundamental for the arm
movements. Because of its importance and because it is
one of most complex parts in a human body in terms
of the geometry, kinematics, and dynamics, it has been
intensively studied in biomechanics[1]. At the same time,
it has been considered promising to mimic its structure to
develop musculoskeletal robot arms because its structure
should contribute in the realization of humanlike sophisti-
cated arm motions. In fact, in recent years, several robot
arms, equipped with mechanisms inspired from the superior
limb girdle, have been developed[2][3][4].
The shoulder joint complex mainly consists of four joints,
namely the sternoclavicular (SC), arcromioclavicular (AC),
scapulothoracic (ST) and glenohumeral (GH) joints, shown
in Fig.1. In particular, the GH and ST joints are difﬁcult to
implement in an engineering way. Because the GH joint has
three degrees-of-freedom (DOFs) , presents high stability and
has a very wide range of movement, the use of conventional
three DOFs joints such as ball joints is unsatisfying. In
addition, because the ST joint is categorized as a functional

Therefore, designing structures, which assure engineering,
biomechanical, and anatomical plausibility at the same time,
remains one of the most fascinating topics in this interdisci-

In this study, we develop a musculoskeletal robot arm
with a humanlike superior limb girdle which can assure both
engineering, biological, and anatomical plausibility in terms
of its kinematics. To realize the wide range of movement
of the GH joint with high stability, we propose a new ball
joint mechanism. In a conventional ball joint, the movement
is stable but the range of movement is greatly reduced by
the retainer which covers more than half of the ball part
of the joint for the stable movement. In the new ball joint
mechanism, the design of retainer is improved to assure
both a stable movement and a wide range of movement. In
regard to the ST joint, we design the scapula and thorax as
a ﬂat planar object and a cylindr oidal object, respectively.
In this design, both the mobility and stability of the planar
scapula part sliding on the cylindroidal thorax are achieved

II. R ELA TED RESEARCHES
In this paper, we classify engineering designs of the
GH joint into two types, called the opened joint and the
closed joint, and we introduce related researches upon this
classiﬁcation. A simple illustration of the human’s GH joint
is shown in the top part of Fig.2. The bottom of Fig.2 shows
examples of opened and the closed joint.
The opened joint, shown in the left bottom of Fig.2, is
a type of joint consisting of a ball part and a cup part,
corresponding to the humeral head and the glenoid fossa,
respectively. For instance, th e musculoskeletal humanoid
robot “Kojiro” developed by Mizuuchi et al. adopted an
opened joint as its GH joint[6]. In addition, “ECCEROBOT”
developed by Marques et al. employed the opened joint with
elastic and inelastic ligaments to stably actuate the joint[4].
Because not only muscles, but also an articular capsule, an
articular labrum, and ligamen ts keep the humeral head on
the glenoid fossa in humans, as shown in the top of Fig.2,
this approach presents high biological plausibility.
The typical advantages of the opened joint are its wide
range of movement and its high anatomical plausibility. Con-
versely, the abarticulation can occur because the ball part can
be dislocated from the cup part. To prevent the abarticulation,
it is necessary to keep pushing the ball part onto the cup part
by actuators. In other words, we must control actuators to
exert not only the joint torque but also the holding force
to use the opened joint. Introduce elastic ligaments can
simplify this problem, however, it is still necessary to face
the original difﬁculty of choosing the elasticity of ligaments.
Furthermore, dealing with this problem in dynamic motions
becomes an increasing challenge.
Instead of the opened joint, many musculoskeletal robots
adopt commercially available joints as the GH joint. In
several musculoskeletal robots, joints which have less than
3 DOFs were adopted and combined to realize 3 DOFs GH
joint[3][7]. For instance, a combination of a universal joint
and a joint for axial rotation can realize a 3 DOFs joint.
However, because the three rota tion centers are mismatched,
the kinematics fundamentally differs from the opened joint
and the GH joint. Therefore, spherical joints, which provide 3
DOFs with the same rotation centers, are a suitable competi-
tor of the opened joint. Fig.2 shows a commercially available
spherical joint. Because the c up part of this kind of joints
covers the ball part more than in the case of opened joint, in
the following we call this type of joints as closed joint. For
instance, in [8], a closed joint was adopted as the GH joint
in the musculoskeletal robot. In contrast with the opened
joint, since in the closed joint the cup part, called retainer,
covers more than half of the ball part, the abarticulation is
perfectly prevented. For these joints the typical advantage is
thus the stability of the movement, while the disadvantage
is the narrow movement range.
From the above-mentioned related researches, it can be
seen that both the opened joint and the closed joint are
still insufﬁcient to support bot h a wide range of movement,
stability of movement, and biological plausibility at the same
time.
The ST joint is a functional joint and allows three
kinds of movements which are elevation/depression, abduc-
tion/adduction, and upward/downward rotation. So far it has
been often modeled in biomechanics as a constraints that
forces two points on the scapula to be on the surface of
the thorax [9]. Although it is known in physiology that
the scapula can leave from the thorax during motions[5],
these models simplify the constraint in order to solve the
kinematics or the dynamics because, even under these sim-
plifying assumptions, the model is very complex. Since
there are several observations and hypotheses about the

Also in robotics, many designs of the ST joint have been

human beings.
Therefore, it can be said that the modeling and the imple-

quantitative evaluations of ST joint’s designs could be found
only in [2] and the measure is the range of movement of the
link corresponding to the humerus.
III. D ESIGN OF MUSCULOSKELETAL ROBOT ARM
Fig.3 shows the the developed musculoskeletal robot arm
and the conﬁguration of its DOFs. This robot arm has a
humanlike superior limb girdle. In addition, excluding the
AC and SC joints, the robot has 7 DOFs, which are similar
to the DOFs present in humans and to the DOFs of previously
developed musculoskeletal robots which are not focusing on
the superior limb girdle[7][8]. In the following subsections,
we will provide details on the designs of the GH joint, the
ST joint, and other parts such as the arm and the hand. At
the end of this section, we also describe the actuators and
control system.
A. Glenohumeral Joint
As explained in last section, the biggest technical problem
in the realization of the GH joint is to provide a wide
range of movement, high movement stability, and biological
plausibility at the same time. To deal with this problem, we
propose a new mechanism, based on a ball joint in which
rotatable claws hold the ball part on the cup part.
Fig.4 shows the three dimensional model of the new ball
joint. The ball part, the hol ding claws, and the cup part
are made from low friction aromatic polyamide plastics to
smoothen the movement. In addition, the size of the joint
is comparable to the human’s GH joint. In this mechanism,
when the shaft connected to the ball part collides to one of
the claws, the claws passively rotate and the shaft can move
into the gap between the claws. Because of this design, we
call this newly developed joint as a partially opened joint.
The most important feature of the partially opened joint is
that the rotation centers of three DOFs coincide as for the GH
joint. Given its structure, it is obvious that the proposed joint
provides both a wide range of movement and high stability
of the movement. In the human GH joint shown in Fig.2,

contacting components corresponding to the scapula and
the thorax, the shapes of the parts determine the contact
condition. Therefore, the shapes should be designed to realize
both the mobility and stability of the scapula part over the
execution of all kinds of movements of the human’s ST
joint which are elevation/depression, abduction/adduction,
and upward/downward rotation. In addition, assuming to
drive the joint by P AMs, the shapes should also imitate the

the clavicle are comparable with those of humans.
To improve the mobility of the scapula part, the contact
pressure should be reduced to smoothen the movement. On
the other hand, to achieve the stability of the movement, the
number of contacting points between the two parts should be
increased. However, as explained in last section, because of
the clavicle (SC and AC joints), providing surface contact on
the curved surface of the thorax is geometrically impossible
if the full scapula motion is permitted. Furthermore, even
if the clavicle is omitted to realize the ST joint, the shapes
of the scapula and the thorax would need to be spherical,
and this would be greatly different from what is seen in
humans. Therefore, providin g a line contact between the ﬂat
planar scapula and the cylindroidal thorax appears a suitable
solution for realizing the ST joint.

D. Actuators and Control System
To drive the developed skeletal structure explained above,
P AMs were employed. Because P AMs have inherent ﬂexi-
bility, it is possible to allocate them freer than it is for other
actuators. Fig.7 shows the conﬁguration of the 25 P AMs. The
conﬁguration is schematically similar to that of humans (e.g.
there are not only monoarticular muscles but also biarticular
muscles). The hand is driven by two P AMs in the manner of
underactuated robotic hands[14].
Each pneumatic artiﬁcial muscle is equipped both with an
internal pressure sensor and an axial tension sensor (except
for muscles No.23 and No.24: these muscles, which are driv-
ing the hand, have only internal pressure sensors). Because
the conﬁguration of the muscles is very complex, mounting
large sensors is spatially difﬁcult. Therefore, this robot arm
employs compact sensors, namely PSE530 pressure sensor
manufactured by SMC, and compact hand-build axial tension
sensors[15].

IV . E XPERIMENT
In this section, we ﬁrst evaluate the range of movement
of the developed musculoskeletal robot arm. The result is
compared with the range of humans and with the results
reported in [2]. Second, we present a throwing motion
executed by the developed robot arm in order to demonstrate
the feasibility of the structure.
A. Range of Movement
The range of movement of the developed robot arm was
measured by adjusting the PID gain of the control system and
the desired pressures in order for the range of movement to
be maximized. Fig.9 shows the postures of the developed
musculoskeletal robot arm that can be taken as extremes of
the ranges of movement. Table I reports the comparisons of
the range of movement among the developed musculoskeletal
robot arm, a human, and the existing musculoskeletal robot
arm shown in [2]. Note that the range of movement of
the developed musculoskeletal robot arm is measured as the
angle between positions assumed by the humerus link when
observed on the lateral plane (abduction/adduction), on the
sagittal plane (ﬂextion/extension) and on the horizontal plane
(horizontal ﬂexion/horizontal extension). As it is technically
difﬁcult to measure displacem ent angles of ball joints, the
angle was measured from the photographs of front view, side
view, and top view of the robot arm. In these results, it can be
seen that the developed musculoskeletal robot arm presents
a similar range of movement for the abduction, adduction
and ﬂexion directions. Additionally, on these directions, the

B. Throwing Motion
In order to demonstrate the feasibility of realizing dynamic
motions, we present a throwing motion of the developed
robot arm. With the developed musculoskeletal robot arm,
sets of desired pressures of the P AMs must be provided to
generate motions. In this paper, we prepared four sets of
desired pressures, corresponding to the initial, the cocked
up, the swung down and the releasing the ball postures. We
then generated a throwing motion by switching from a set
of pressures to the successive at opportune time intervals.

V. C ONCLUSION
The design of the humanlike shoulder complex for mus-
culoskeletal robot arms was proposed in this research. We
ﬁrst proposed a new ball joint mechanism, called partially
opened joint, which gives the GH joint both a wide range
of movement and high stability. Second, we presented a
humanlike ST joint consisting of a ﬂat planar object, a
cylindroidal object, and a rod connecting the two objects
which correspond to the scapula, the thorax, and the clavicle
of humans, respectively. To drive the developed skeletal
structure containing the partially opened joint and the hu-
manlike ST joint, P AMs were used because their inherent
ﬂexibility allows freer allocations of actuators than conven-
tional actuators such as electrical motors. In other words,
P AMs can be located according to muscles present in the
human superior limb. To validate the proposed designs, the
range of movement was compared with that of a human and
of an existing robot arm. In addition, we showed that the
developed musculoskeletal robot arm can dynamically throw
a ball thanks to the wide movable areas of joints and the
ﬂexibility of P AMs.
