Design features in human joints with recommendations for
humanoid robot design
Stuart C Burgess∗
School of Electrical, Electronic and Mechanical Engineering, Faculty of Science and Engineering, University of Bristol, Bristol, United

Keywords: humanoid robotics, synovial joints, multifunctioning, design strategies
Abstract
There are many types of moveable joint in the human body, with each characterised by compact-
ness and high functionality. In contrast, humanoid robots typically use just three or four simple
types of joint that are relatively bulky with limited functionality. Therefore, there is significant
potential for bioinspired design. The paper describes 14 different types of moveable joint in the
human musculoskeletal system and describes important design features such as linkage mecha-
nisms, suspension systems and segmentation. In each case, recent examples of bioinspired design
or potential for bioinspired design are given. Results are tabulated for ease of reference by design-
ers. The paper highlights key future goals for humanoid robotics such as replicating the MTP
joints of the foot, replicating the push-retract motion in fingers and replicating the compact link-
age pronation mechanism of the wrist. Human joints exhibit design principles that are quite differ-
ent to those of traditional engineering such as high degrees of freedom, redundant actuation and
multisensory integration. These strategies have potential to inspire a different and more effective
approach to the mechanical design of humanoid robots.
1.Introduction
Human joints generally have superior performance to
those of humanoid robots in terms of degrees of free-
dom (DoF), range of motion, compactness, energy
efficiency and control [ 1–3]. This paper reviews the
moveable joints in the human musculoskeletal sys-
tem in order to highlight features and design prin-
ciples that can potentially improve the performance
of humanoid robots. In this paper, human moveable
joints are classified into 14 different types includ-
ing three types of segmented joint. For each type
of joint, the design rationale is given together with
examples of bioinspired designs or potential for
bioinspiration.
When considering optimality in joints, account
must be taken that what is optimal for animal joints
may not be optimal for robot joints because of dif-
ferent constraints and objectives. For example, ani-
mal joints are assembled and integrated from the bot-
tom up and are living systems with constant renewing
and repair of materials. However, with modern tech-
nologies, the difference in constraints is narrowing.
Manufacturing methods such as 3D printing now
make it possible to construct joints in a bottom-up
process [ 4]. This makes it more feasible to achieve
complex geometries and integration that were pre-
viously not possible. In addition, there have been
recent significant advances in self-healing robots [ 5].
For low-cost robots with modest performance objec-
tives, there may be limited potential for bioinspiration
because of the complexity of human joints. However,
for high-performance humanoid robots, bioinspira-
tion offers potential for significant improvements in
capability.
Mammals are well known to have superior per-
formance to robots in areas such as balance, locomo-
tion and hand dexterity [ 1–3]. Of course, robots can
be highly efficient and superior to animals in some
well-defined tasks. However, for dynamic move-
ments, mammals are often far superior in terms of
speed, agility and range, especially in unstructured

environments. Key examples of agility include: catch-
ing, climbing, sprinting, leaping, and being able
to quickly change running direction. Such actions
require fast, precise and coordinated movements of
multiple joints.
T able1 gives a summary comparison of the design
and performance of locomotion and hand dexterity of
humans and some commercially available humanoid
robots. The human musculoskeletal system has a
much wider range of joint designs with 10 out of
14 joints having multiple DoF . In contrast, humanoid
robots typically use just three or four types of joint
(hinge, pivot, linear and spherical) with just one type
(spherical) having more than one degree of freedom
[6–8]. Humans also have a far higher quantity of
joints and actuators. In addition, the human body
has a far higher number of sensors (e.g. propriocep-
tors and touch sensors), a more powerful process-
ing centre (the human brain), more advanced control
strategies (e.g. multisensory integration and redun-
dant actuation) and a far higher level of mechanical
and control integration [1–3]. These factors combine
to give much higher levels of agility in locomotion
and dexterity in hands.
Human feet play a major role in bipedal loco-
motion because they act as a stiff lever during push-
off and then reconfigure into a flexible structure for
landing [ 12]. Current robot feet have limited func-
tionality compared to human feet in three areas:
(i) Ankle joint
There are three rotational DoF in the human
ankle that allows for significant changes of direc-
tion in running [ 9]. In contrast, commercial
humanoid robots, such as those in table 1,
have a single DoF ankle joint (plantar flexion-
dorsiflexion) which allows for more gradual
changes in direction.
(ii) MTP joints
The human foot has a major joint at the base of
the toes (MTP joints) that enables the front of
the foot to act as a launch pad [ 9, 13]. In con-
trast, robot feet are generally flat with no toes or
toe joints. The main problem with flat feet is that
there is line contact with the ground at push-off
which is difficult to control, especially on uneven
ground. The use of spring-loaded MTP joints
has recently been adopted in some robots like
Optimus2 [6] but no active MTP joints are cur-
rently used on commercial robots.
(iii) Shock absorption
The arched human foot acts as a shock absorber
mainly through stretching of the ligaments [ 14].
In contrast, robot feet are generally stiff with a
simple rubber pad that has limited shock absorb-
ing capacity. The biomechanics of the feet is
addressed in more detail in section 2.5.
The gap in locomotion performance between
humans and current robots can be illustrated in activ-
ities like running. In April 2025 there was the first offi-
cial example of humanoid robots competing along-
side humans in a running race on smooth tarmac
at the Yizhuang half marathon in Beijing. Around
21 humanoid robots joined around 12 000 runners in
the half-marathon with the robots having their own
dedicated lane. The plan is for robots to compete each
year in the annual Yizhuang half marathon. In the
2025 inaugural race, the fastest robot finished in a
time of 2 h and 40 min giving an average speed of
around 7.9 km h −1 [15]. The winner of the men’s
race had a time of 1 h and 2 min, giving an aver-
age speed of 20.4 km h −1. Since the 2025 Yizhuang
half marathon was only the first race where robots
have competed against humans, it is almost certain
that robots will achieve significantly improved perfor-
mances in future years.
Of course, the times of human runners vary
greatly depending on levels of ability and training. In
one study of females aged 25 (an age where peak per-
formance would be expected), average half marathon
times have been calculated from past race results for
five levels: beginners, novice, intermediate, advanced
and elite [ 16]. Beginners ran an average time of 2 h
and 43 min whereas intermediate runners ran an
average time of 2 h [ 16]. This means that the fastest
humanoid robot in the 2025 Yizhuang half marathon
was 3 min faster than the average beginner’s time,
and 40 min slower than the average intermediate
time for a 25-year-old female. Therefore, there is
already overlapping of performance between robots
and humans.
It should be noted that if the Yizhuang half
marathon course had involved rough terrain instead
of smooth tarmac, then the humanoid robots would
have been far slower than the fastest humans because
robots have inferior stability to humans when run-
ning on uneven surfaces [10, 11].
The gap in performance between humans and
robots is even greater when considering unstructured
activities like team football. Robots and AI strategies
have been developed that enable bipedal robots to
play football in real time [ 17]. In 2025 there was a
three-a-side autonomous AI robot football competi-
tion in China consisting of two 10-min halves [ 18].
The level of football skill was very basic with the
ball being kicked very slowly along the ground. The
speed at which the robots moved was around an order
of magnitude slower than with professional human
footballers.

humanoid robotic hands in terms of range of motion,
precision and speed of movement [3]. A key measure
of performance of hands is the linear push-retract
motion (writing movement) of fingers which involves
simultaneous flexion and extension of different joints

In order to close the gap in performance with
humans, improvements are required in all areas of
mechanical design including joints, actuators, sen-
sors, control and integration [1–3]. One way to make
improvements is to understand the biomechanics of
human joints and to see where bioinspired design is
beneficial and appropriate. There is a long history
of engineers being inspired by the mechanical design
solutions found in nature. In 1877 John George W ood
published the book Nature’s Teachings with around
750 diagrams comparing mechanical design in nature
with engineering [ 19]. In 1917 D’arcy Thompson
published the first edition of his classic book On
Growth and Form showing that biological forms are
often governed by laws of physics and mathematics
[20]. At the end of the twentieth century there were
several significant books published on mechanical
design in nature including by Wainright in 1976 [21],
Julian Vincent in 1982 [22], Adrian Bejan in 2000 [23]
and Steven V ogel in 2003 [24]. There have been recent
research studies describing design features in natu-
ral materials [ 25], structures [ 26], coatings [ 27] and
sensors [28]. However, not much attention has been
paid to mechanical design features in joints.
The remainder of the paper is organised as fol-
lows. Section 2 describes the design and function of
14 different types of moveable joint found in the
human musculoskeletal system and gives examples
of bioinspired joints or potential for bioinspiration.
These results are tabulated so they can be used as
a reference for designers. Section 3 describes seven
main types of movement in the fingers and how
these are achieved with tendons. Section 4 describes
some of the key design features in synovial lubri-
cation and connective elements (i.e. ligaments and
tendons). Section 5 describes key design features of
muscle and control strategies used for moving joints.
Section 6 discusses lessons for future bioinspired
design of humanoid robots including outlining gen-
eral design strategies that are found in biology.
2.Jointkinematics
2.1.Hingejoints
Figure 1 summarises three types of hinge joint found
in the human musculoskeletal system. Pivot joints are
dealt with separately in section2.2. Even though hinge
joints and pivot joints are both rotary joints, they
are dealt with separately because this is the common
practice in anatomy literature [29].
(1) Hinge joint with fixed centre of rotation
The simple single DoF hinge joint in the human
skeleton is typically formed by the cartilage-
covered convex head on one bone and the
cartilage-covered concave groove on the mating
bone. The hinge joint is also called a ginglymus
joint. Typical examples are found in the inter-
phalangeal joints of the fingers and toes. The
hinge joint is typically constrained by collateral
ligaments and there is typically a joint capsule
containing synovial fluid. The joints are actuated

via tendons that act like cables (see section 3).
Robotic fingers also commonly employ hinge
joints although they generally use a pin-and-hole
design with lugs rather than ligaments to con-
strain the hinge [ 30]. The biological ligament
design is more compact and robust because the
design is much simpler and contains a degree of
elasticity. Therefore, there is potential for bioin-
spired design in this area.
(2) Linkage hinge joint with moving centre of rotation
The mammalian knee is a type of suspended hinge
joint with a significant moving centre of rota-
tion as shown in figure 1. The knee is considered
to have six DoF , with flexion-extension and axial
rotation being the most significant movements
[31]. The main advantage of the moving centre of
rotation is that it enables the femur to roll around
the tibia and thus produce a large range of motion
(over 150 degrees in a young adult). Without the
rolling motion, the range of motion would be sig-
nificantly lower because the tibia and femur bones
have large radii at the joint interface which would
restrict range of motion if there was a fixed centre
of rotation. The large radii are necessary because
of the large loads that the joint experiences. The
rolling motion is possible because the knee has
an inverted four-bar linkage system to guide the
movement of the joint, as shown in figure 1. The
two crossed links are formed by the two cruciate
ligaments and the two parallel bars are effectively
formed by the tibia and femur bones. The motion
of the four-bar mechanism must closely match
the rolling motion of the joint to avoid jamming.
As well as high range of movement in flexion-
extension and a moving centre of rotation, the
knee is also capable of significant axial rota-
tion around the tibia axis due to the geome-
try of the tibia plateau and meniscus. Therefore,
the knee has at least three significant DoF [ 31].
The high DoF of the knee joint enable humans
to run and change direction with high levels of
agility [31]. A bioinspired knee joint that includes
condyles and ligaments has been developed that
mimics the flexion-extension and axial rotation
of the human knee [ 32]. A bioinspired robotic
knee joint with ligaments and adjustable stiff-
ness is given in [ 33]. These bioinspired exam-
ples enable humanoid robots to have increased
agility.
(3) Suspended hinge joint with planar movement
The two temporomandibular joints (TMJ) in the
jaw combine a hinge joint with bilateral planar
movement by suspending the lower jaw via articu-
lar discs as shown in figure1. The TMJ is classed as
a ‘ginglymoarthrodial’ joint since it is both a ging-
lymus (hinging joint) and an arthrodial (sliding)
joint. The freedom of movement in the jaw joints
gives three main DoF and allows for the complex
chewing motion of the mouth which involves pla-
nar motions and hinge motion. The two artic-
ular discs are situated between the condyles in
the lower jaw and the notches in the skull and
these enable the lower jaw to be pulled laterally
by muscles.
There is a demand for robots that can simu-
late human chewing in order to test dental products.
However, there are not many robots that can accu-
rately simulate human jaw motion [ 34]. One robotic
jaw, using a six-bar mechanism, has achieved high
accuracy in mimicking jaw motion. However, it has
a bulkier design than the human jaw [35]. In terms of
humanoid robots, there may not be a need to accu-
rately replicate human jaw movement. However, the
TMJ joints give a good example of how an actively
controlled suspension system can give multiple DoF
in a joint.
2.2.Pivotjoints
Pivot joints are defined here as a shaft in a sleeve joint.
T wo types of pivot joints are summarised in figure 2.
(1) Shaft-sleeve pivot joint
An example of a simple pivot joint (trochoid
joint) is found between the C1 (atlas) and C2
(axis) vertebrae at the top of the spinal column
that enables the head to turn left and right as
shown in figure 2. The C2 vertebra has a round
protrusion (odontoid process) that fits into a hole
in the C1 vertebra that is formed by bone and a lig-
ament. The C1 vertebra is fixed to the head whilst
the C2 vertebra supports the head rotation. Pivot
joints are common in robotics.
(2) Linkage pivot joint
Supination and pronation of the wrist joint is an
example of a ‘linkage pivot joint’ where a bone
(the radius) pivots (against the humerus) at the
same time as moving in three dimensions through
a conical path as part of a linkage mechanism. The
ulna serves as a stationary central pillar for rota-
tion of the radius. In contrast, the radius is able
to pivot at the joint with the humerus to allow
it to rotate around the ulna as shown in figure 2.
Because the hand is fixed to the radius, the hand
rotates as the radius rotates.
During pronation, the radius physically crosses
over the ulna, changing the parallel structure into
an ‘ X’ shape as shown in figure 2. The two bones
are held together for stability by the interosseous
membrane, a dense connective tissue sheet that also
helps transfer forces between them. The ulna, radius
and connecting ligaments can be approximately mod-
elled by a four-bar linkage mechanism [ 36]. A very

2.3.Linearjoints
Linear (planar) joints include unilinear and bilinear
joints. Figure 3 shows three types of linear joint in the
human musculoskeletal system.
(1) Planar joints
The simple sliding planar joint is typically formed
by flat or slightly curved surfaces of adjacent
cartilage-covered bones. Planar joints in mam-
mals are often bilateral such as with the joints in
the wrists and ankles. An important planar joint
in the ankle is the subtalar joint that helps enable
pronation [39]. Planar joints are also referred to as
arthrodial or gliding joints. Linear joints are com-
mon in robotics in linear actuators.

(2) Linkage tie rod joint
Because the foot takes the weight of the whole
body, the ankle joint must carry high loads during
pronation. A key function of the fibula bone is to
act as a tie rod in applying a linear stabilising force
onto the side of the ankle [40]. The fibula motion
can be approximately modelled as a four-bar
mechanism with the tibia and fibula represented
by two long bars and connecting ligaments rep-
resented by two short bars as shown in figure 3.
This four-bar concept models the gliding motion
of the fibula [ 40]. The gliding motion of the
fibula can also be modelled using a fully parallel
mechanism with five bars [ 41]. The fibula allows
fine-tuning of the moment arm (for mechanical
advantage) for the muscles acting on the ankle-
foot complex. A second function of the fibula is to
increase the attachment area for muscles, allowing
more muscle to act on the joint. A recent bioin-
spired ankle design has been produced that mim-
ics the motion of the ankle joint although it does
not contain a fibula tie rod connection [ 42].

(3) Suspended anchor joint
The hyoid bone is a fully suspended and isolated
bone that is connected to several (linear actua-
tor) muscles as shown in figure 3. The hyoid bone
plays a crucial role in swallowing, tongue move-
ment and speech [ 43]. The hyoid bone is located
just below the mouth and above the larynx and
is suspended by a network of muscles, ligaments
and cartilage that tether it to surrounding struc-
tures like the mandible. Muscles go up from the
hyoid to the tongue and floor of the oral cavity.
Other muscles go down to the larynx whilst oth-
ers go across to the epiglottis and pharynx. There
are at least eight muscles that form a tensegrity
structure. One advantage of the hyoid bone being
suspended is that it helps to co-ordinate mus-
cles in actions like swallowing [ 43]. Swallowing
requires precise co-ordination of muscle actions
to move the epiglottis and open the oesophageal
sphincter for food passage at the right time.
Because the hyoid is suspended, this means that
fine control of the muscles is required. In terms
of humanoid robots, there may not be a need
to copy the hyoid bone for the same functions.
However, the hyoid bone gives a good example
of how a high number of DoF can be achieved in
a joint through an actively controlled suspension
system.

2.4.Sphericalandasphericaljoints
Spherical and aspherical joints are important because
they give multiple DoF . Figure4 shows three types of
spherical and aspherical joints in the human muscu-
loskeletal system.
(1) Ball and socket joint
In the case of a ball and socket joint, the ball is
usually close to spherical, thus giving three DoF
in rotation. The hip and shoulder are two impor-
tant ball and socket joints. The shoulder joint
has a shallower socket that gives greater range
of motion but less stability. The hip joint needs
greater stability for the weight-bearing activities
of running and jumping. Ball-socket type joints
are common in engineering, although the socket
usually takes the form of a relatively narrow
curved sleeve. An example of a musculoskeletal
humanoid robot using ball and socket joints is
given in [ 44]. This robot uses twisted and coiled
polymer actuators and the robot is largely 3D
printed.
(2) Ellipsoidal joint
An ellipsoidal joint (condyloid joint) differs from
a ball and socket joint in that the bearing surfaces
are ellipsoidal and not spherical. This means an
ellipsoidal joint has two degrees freedom in rota-
tion, making it a biaxial joint. An advantage of an
ellipsoidal joint over a ball and socket joint is that
it can have line contact rather than point contact.
This can make an ellipsoidal joint more stable and
stronger than a ball and socket joint for the same
volume. An example of an ellipsoid joint is the
radiocarpal joint of the wrist that allows flexion-
extension as well as abduction-adduction and cir-
cumduction. In the case of the radiocarpal joint,
the radius bone has a concave ovoid articular sur-
face and the carpal bones have an elliptical cavity
(see figure 4). The knuckle joints of the hand also
have ellipsoid joints.
An interesting design feature in the wrist is
that of matching radii in the two joints of
flexion-extension. During flexion-extension, slid-
ing movements occur at both the radiocarpal
and midcarpal joints. The two hinge joints cre-
ate the potential for two centres of rotation and
chaotic motion unless the joints have a common
centre of rotation. This common centre of rota-
tion is shown in figure 4, with a circular sym-
bol. The midcarpal joint has a radius smaller than
the radiocarpal joint and by a ratio that does
give an approximate common centre of rotation
[45]. A bioinspired cable-driven spherical wrist
joint using a ball joint for robots is described
in [46].
(3) Saddle joint
A saddle joint differs from an ellipsoidal joint
in that the two surfaces are not convex-concave
but both convex. This gives more freedom of
movement and a greater range of movement. One
potential disadvantage is that of lower load capac-
ity due to the contact area being more of a point
contact than line contact. A prime example of a
saddle joint is that of the thumb which requires
a range of motion in order to make pinch grips
with each finger. Emulating the functionality of
the thumb is a key goal for humanoid robotics
because of the importance of the pinch grip in
hand tasks. T wo recent examples of bioinspired
thumbs with high range of motion using soft
actuators are given in [47, 48].
2.5.Segmentedjoints
When multiple joints are linked together into a seg-
mented assembly, they can exhibit a high num-
ber of DoF and perform more complex move-
ments. Segmented joints are also ideal for perform-
ing multiple functions. Figure 5 shows three types
of segmented joints in the human musculoskeletal
system.
(1) Segmented arches
The 12 bones of the hind and midfoot (seven
ankle bones and five metatarsals) form three
arches as shown in figure 5. The medial lon-
gitudinal arch passes through the three biggest
metatarsals and the ankle. The lateral longitudinal
arch passes through the two smallest metatarsals
and through the ankle. The transverse arch joins
the two longitudinal arches, particularly at the
ball of the feet at the MTP joints. These arches play
a very important role in bipedal standing, walk-
ing and running [ 14]. Each of the arches is like a
Roman block arch with wedge-shaped bones and
including a keystone. However, unlike a Roman
arch, the arches of the foot are able to flex under
load due to stretching of ligaments. The arches
have four key functions. (i) Stiff lever for push-
off. When running, the medial longitudinal arch
becomes a stiff lever due to tightening of muscles
and ligaments [49] and due to the stiffening effect
of the transverse arch [ 50]. (ii) The foot tran-
sitions to being three flexible arches for landing
[12]. The flexibility is due to elasticity of liga-
ments such as the plantar fascia and the spring
ligament [ 14]. (iii) The transverse arch (the ball
of the foot) acts as a fulcrum during running. (iv)
The three arches give three-point contact with the
ground to enable standing on one leg as shown
in figure 5. These capabilities of the human foot
represent key goals for future humanoid robots.

(2) Segmented levers
The five toes of the foot represent five small
flexible levers and play an important multifunc-
tional role in standing, walking and running. One
function of the toes, especially the big toe, is to
act as stiff levers and form a launch pad in the
final phase of push-off in walking and running
as shown in figure 5 [53, 54]. A second function
of the toes is to provide stability during standing,
walking and running [ 55]. The toes have enough
movement and flexibility to conform to uneven
ground, especially when barefoot. The flexibility
of toes also helps humans to change direction in
walking and running [55]. A third function of the
toes is to give a contact patch with the ground that
allows for standing on the toes. These capabilities
of toes represent key goals for future humanoid
robots.
(3) Segmented column
The spinal column has cartilaginous joints that
have limited movement compared to synovial
joints. However, even though movement in one
vertebral cartilaginous joint is small, the com-
bined effect of many segmented vertebrae can
produce significant movements [ 56] especially
when combined with movements at the hips and
shoulders. Professional gymnasts can commonly
arch their back forwards and backwards through
180 degrees. The spinal column, as an assembly of
joints, has three rotational DoF: bending in two
planes and twisting around the axis of the spinal
column. The segmented system of joints creates
a strong and robust structure at the same time
as providing flexibility. There are approximately
33 vertebrae (at least 24 of which are moveable),

3.Fingerkinematics
The fingers have three joints with four DoF and mul-
tiple tendons that enable finely controlled and com-
plex movements. Each finger has at least six muscles
controlling its movement: three extrinsic and three
intrinsic muscles. The index and little fingers have an
extra indicis extensor, that enables pointing. Figure 6
shows a schematic of the anatomy of the index fin-
ger together with key finger movements. Each indi-
vidual joint can be flexed and extended because there
are tendon connections on each of the three fin-
ger bones on the extension and flexion sides. These
act as antagonistic pairs. There are also interossei
muscles that abduct and adduct the fingers. There
are also ligaments that play a role in finger move-
ments such as the oblique retinacular ligament [ 59].
The exact biomechanics of tendons and ligaments in
finger kinematics is not fully understood and subject
to ongoing research [59]. There are seven main types
of finger movement that are of interest to robotics
engineers:
(1–3) Three flexion and extension movements
Humans can flex and extend their fingers in at
least three ways as shown in figure 6. Full flex-
ion is a very important type of finger move-
ment because it is used for gripping and hold-
ing objects. Hand grips are so important that
babies are born with a palmer grasp reflex
that is triggered when the palm is stimulated
by touch. Partial flexion-extensions are more
complicated to perform because some joints
have to be kept straight whilst others are flexed
and extended. Some people can also indepen-
dently flex the last phalange bone. Many robot
hands focus on full flexion and full extension in
the fingers [60].
(4) Push and retract movement (combined flexion
and extension)
The push-retract motion of fingers (see
figure 6) is performed for intricate activities
that require a linear motion such as writing
and threading a needle. It is a very complex
movement because it involves simultaneous
flexion and extension at different joints in the
same finger. This complex movement requires
multiple tendons to be pulled simultaneously.
The simultaneous combination of flexion and
extension is possible because the lumbrical
passes the knuckle joint on the palmer side
but then passes over the PIP and DIP joints on
the dorsal side as shown in figure 6. When the
lumbrical contracts, it simultaneously causes
flexion at the MCP joint and extension at the
PIP and DIP joints, thus moving the finger
forwards in a pushing motion. T o retract the
finger, multiple tendons are pulled such as the
central slip of the central extensor tendon and
the flexor digitorum profundis (FDP) tendon.
The longer length of the FDP flexor tendon and
the relative moment arms of the tendons to the
joints, helps to produce the retract motion.
This review has found no robotic hand that
can reproduce the push-retract motion. This
capability of human fingers represents a key
goal for future humanoid robots.
(5) Abduction-adduction
Abduction and adduction are important
motions for activities such as making ges-
tures and holding round, large objects in one
hand. The interossei muscles cause abduc-
tion and adduction at the two DoF ellipsoidal
metacarpal phalangeal (MCPJ) joints. Many
robotic hands have been developed that have
some degree of abduction-adduction [ 61, 62].
(6) Circumduction
Circumduction is caused by a combination
of flexion/abduction/extension/adduction at
the knuckle joint. T o produce circumduction
at constant speed is challenging because this
involves at least four different finger muscles
moving with varying speed and with pre-
cise co-ordination. An example of a robot
that can perform circumduction is given
in [63].
(7) Pinch grips
The thumb can form a pinch grip with each
finger, including a tripod grip (between the
thumb, index finger and middle finger) which
is ideal for holding a pen or tool. The hand
can also perform simultaneous pinch grips
(e.g. between the thumb and index finger and
between the ring and little finger) in activi-
ties like knitting. A recently developed 19 DoF
robotic hand can perform pinch grips using five
pairs of shape memory alloy (SMA) actuators
[64]. There have been at least three differ-
ent approaches in the design of robotic hands.
Some use linkage-driven fingers [62, 65], whilst
others opt for cable-driven fingers [ 66]. In
recent times there has been a trend for soft
robotic fingers [ 67, 68]. Another important
feature of human hand dexterity is the role
of sensors for giving feedback on parame-
ters such as contact pressures [ 69]. There are
many thousands of touch sensors and nerve
endings on each hand that sense pressure,
movement and vibration. An example of a
bioinspired hand with touch sensors is given
in [70].

4.Lubricationandconnectingelements
4.1.Synoviallubrication
Synovial fluid is a clear viscous substance that lubri-
cates articular surfaces, transports nutrients to car-
tilage cells and removes waste products. Depending
on the load level, load duration and joint movement,
different tribological mechanisms of lubrication take
place. The tribological mechanisms of synovial lubri-
cation are complex and still under investigation [ 71].
Synovial lubrication leads to friction coefficients as
low as µ ≈ 0.002, even at high pressures [ 72]. This is
at least an order of magnitude better than is achieved
in sliding surfaces in robotic and prosthetic joints.
12

===== PAGE 14 =====
Bioinspir. Biomim.21 (2026) 041005 S C Burgess
Some of the mechanisms of synovial lubrication are
as follows.
(1) Lubricin boundary lubrication
The synovial fluid constituent responsible for the
boundary lubrication is a glycoprotein, termed
‘lubricin’ . Lubricin has a brush-like molecular
structure which allows lubricin to self-assemble a
monolayer of lubricant molecules onto both car-
tilage surfaces [73]. This macromolecular mono-
layer on each surface forms a repulsive interface.
The two layers (1–100 nm in thickness) reduce
friction and help to prevent cartilaginous wear by
impeding the direct contact of surfaces.
(2) Thin-film hydration lubrication
In fluid-film lubrication mode, the two sliding
surfaces are separated by a fluid film with a
thickness larger than the surface asperities. The
fluid layer consists of phospholipids which have
hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails. The
structure of the phospholipids allows them to
self-assemble onto the cartilage surface to form
a microscopic lipid-based boundary [ 74]. This
results in a low friction slip plane. Frictional dissi-
pation takes place through shear of the hydration
layers at the slip plane.
(3) Weeping lubrication
Articular cartilage has a porous collagenous
matrix structure filled with synovial fluid (inter-
stitial fluid). When the joint is under load, the car-
tilage is compressed which forces interstitial fluid
out of the pore holes in the cartilage and into the
space between the two bones. The interstitial fluid
is hydrostatically pressurised which forms a thin
film that separates the two bones [75]. When load
is removed, interstitial fluid is reabsorbed into
cartilage. The hydrostatic pressure results in very
low friction at low speed [75]. This is very signifi-
cant because achieving low friction at low speeds
is challenging in engineered systems due to factors
such as higher viscous drag.
At present there is not a strong demand for ultra-
low sliding friction in robot joints because of the
use of rolling contact ball bearings. However, ball
bearings come at the cost of a bulky and complex
design. In order to achieve compactness and ultra-low
weight, there is likely to be a future drive to remove
ball bearings and adopt low sliding friction technolo-
gies. An additional motivation for having ultra-low
friction is a desire to make robotic parts long lasting.
Interestingly, synovial fluid joints match the perfor-
mance of rolling contact bearings in terms of coeffi-
cient of friction, so this demonstrates that rolling con-
tact bearings are not essential.
It is very challenging to mimic synovial lubri-
cation because biological systems are living systems
where synovial fluid is constantly being renewed.
One successful area of bioinspiration has been in
the development of hydrogels that replicate hydra-
tion lubrication [ 76–78]. These hydrogels are often
composed of sodium hyaluronate and a bottle
brush polymer which provides a synergistic inter-
action that enhances lubrication and wear resis-
tance. There has also been progress in develop-
ing porous materials and surfaces that can hold
lubricants [79].
4.2.Connectivetissues(ligamentsandtendons)
Ligaments join bones and guide joint motion whilst
the primary function of tendons is to join muscle to
bone. Efficient connections play a vital role in pro-
ducing compact and high-performance joints. The
importance of ligaments and tendons is illustrated by
their quantity. There are several hundred ligaments in
the human body and at least 1200 tendons since each
muscle has at least two tendons. Some of the impor-
tant design features in ligaments and tendons are
as follows:
(1) Soft structures
T raditional mechanical connecting elements like
bolts, bearings and cables are generally rigid. In
contrast, the ligaments and tendons in animal
joints are made of soft tissue and are able to bend
and stretch. Ligaments and tendons are com-
posed of longitudinally arranged collagen fibres,
elastin, glycoproteins and proteoglycans which
results in a tough, strong and flexible connecting
element. In general, ligaments are more flexible
than tendons due to a higher degree of elastin.
The higher flexibility of ligaments is more suited
to the role of guiding joints whilst the higher stiff-
ness of tendons is more suited to transmission of
force. Ligaments and tendons have an undulating
arrangement of collagen fibres within the extra-
cellular matrix which allows them to absorb and
release strain energy and act as shock absorbers.
A recent example of a bioinspired soft ligament
has been produced by integrating SMA wires
with fishing lines [ 80]. The combination of SMA
wires and polyethylene fibres gives the ligament
human-like mechanical properties including flex-
ibility and self-healing. When the ligament under-
goes stress-induced elongation or relaxation, the
SMA component can be activated via electrical
stimulation to restore the ligament to its original
state.
(2) Scalable hierarchical structures
One of the reasons why ligaments and tendons
are so scalable and robust is that they have a

hierarchical structure. The collagen fibres are
arranged in bundles called fascicles. Groups of
fascicles are joined together to produce the whole
ligament or tendon. This hierarchical structure
means that ligaments and tendons can be formed
across a wide scale range. A bioinspired ligament
has been produced using 3D fibres braided from
polyethene multifilament which reproduces the
nonlinear behaviour of ligaments and tendons
[81]. Four braided bands have been produced
showing that a range of stiffness levels can be
produced.
(3) Graded material properties
The composite structure of ligaments and ten-
dons means that material properties, such as
Y oung’s modulus, can vary according to the needs
of the application. The material properties are
fine-tuned through adjusting the orientation and
quantity of constituent materials such elastin
[82]. One important example of varying material
properties is at the junction of ligament and bone.
This junction is a specialised interface referred
to as an insertion site or enthesis junction. The
junction consists of fibrocartilage that has a struc-
turally continuous gradient from uncalcified liga-
ment to calcified bone, producing optimal merg-
ing of the soft ligament into hard bone [ 83]. The
thickness of the enthesis is typically around 500
µm [ 84]. An additional feature that strengthens
the interface is that of radii at the junction of
the ligament and bone. The radii help to avoid
stress concentrations and increase the area of the
interface. The enthesis junction results in a very
compact and efficient design. A similar situation
exists for tendon joints with muscle and bone with
grading of material properties.
The strength, flexibility and merged connec-
tions of ligaments and tendons is a key reason
why animal joints are so compact and multi-
functioning. However, it is extremely challeng-
ing to replicate the performance of ligaments
and tendons, especially in the way they are
seamlessly integrated and assembled with bone.
However, technologies such as additive manufac-
ture are now beginning to make merged inter-
faces possible. In one study, a metal to polymer
interlocking design was produced using additive
manufacture [85].
(4) Adaptive growth
Ligaments and tendons can adapt in size when
loads are increased gradually over several months.
For example, when athletes gradually increase
loads on their joints, ligaments and tendons
increase in size to withstand higher loads
[86].
5.Actuationandcontrol
5.1.Muscle
(1) Soft structure
Whereas traditional engineering actuators are
stiff and often need gearing, animal muscle is
compliant with high stain rates that make gear-
ing unnecessary. The compliance of animal mus-
cle enables muscle to perform multiple functions
beyond actuation. The compliance of muscle
means that animal joints can withstand impacts
and enforced displacements. Animal life often
involves close interactions with objects and other
animals which would be problematic with stiff
joints. The compliance of animal muscle also has
the advantage that it can be formed into an almost
infinite variety of shapes so that it can fit around
any joint in the body. A further advantage is that
muscle can give shape and padding to the body.
The ideal design of animal muscle has led to a very
large research effort into the development of soft
actuators for robots [87, 88].
(2) Hierarchical and scalable
Like ligaments, muscle is scalable and robust
because it has a hierarchical structure. The small-
est unit of muscle structure consists of myofil-
ament proteins (actin and myosin) that inter-
act to cause contraction at the molecular level.
Myofilaments are organised into functional units
called sarcomeres which are then combined to
form an individual muscle fibre called myofib-
ril. Separate muscle fibres are grouped together
into fascicles and separate fascicles are grouped
together to form the whole muscle. The small
functional muscle units can work effectively
across a wide scale range. Sarcomeres are approx-
imately 2 µm long and can be assembled to build
muscle with great variations in length and diam-
eter. The stapedius muscle in the ear is the order
of 1 mm in length whereas the sartorius muscle
in the thigh can be over 500 mm in length. The
sphincter muscles of the eye are just 0.1 mm in
thickness whereas the quadriceps femoris in the
thigh is several centimetres in thickness. Recent
developments of fibrous-like and hierarchical soft
actuators are described in [ 89].
(3) Graded properties
Despite the fact that muscles are characterised
by a very similar basic structure, muscle is not
a homogeneous tissue [ 90]. Skeletal myocytes
can be classified into slow-twitch and fast-
twitch fibres. Fast-twitch fibres can be further
subdivided into two additional subclassifica-
tions: (i) fast oxidative for sustained fast aero-
bic metabolism and (ii) fast glycolytic for fast

===== PAGE 16 =====
Bioinspir. Biomim.21 (2026) 041005 S C Burgess
anaerobic metabolism. Different proportions of
these muscle fibre types can be found in all skele-
tal muscles. The diversity in composition allows
skeletal muscles to be fine-tuned to perform a
wide range of movements. There has been recent
progress in producing soft actuators with graded
properties [91, 92].
(4) Adaptive structure
Animal muscle can adapt to changing require-
ments by adapting in size, length and type. For
example, when athletes gradually increase their
training load, their muscles are able to adapt and
grow in size (hypertrophy) and load capability
[93]. After injury, muscle tendon units usually
have reduced range of motion. However, after
repeated stretching exercises, the range of motion
can be restored. The muscle length increase is due
to collagen increasing in alignment in response to
stretching [93]. Muscle fibres also have the ability
to heal and even convert to different fibre types to
adapt to new muscle functions [ 94]. There have
been recent advances in adaptable robotic actua-
tors. An actuator has been developed for an ankle
exoskeleton that adapts stiffness in response to
changes in loading [ 95]. Also, an SMA actuator
has been developed that can be trained to per-
form different actuation tasks involving a range
of forces and motions [96].
5.2.Controlstrategies
Having joints with high DoF requires advanced con-
trol strategies [ 97]. This section describes some key
control strategies that particularly relate to joint
control.
(1) Positive feedback
One example of positive feedback in a mechan-
ical biological system is found in vaginal birth
where dilation of the birth canal is a self-
reinforcing process, i.e. the greater the dilation,
the greater the signals to dilate. A second exam-
ple on a microscale is a switching mechanism
in protein bi-stability that enables the protein
to take up two different stable states [ 98]. A
bioinspired optomechanical soft robotic actua-
tor has been developed using the principle of
positive feedback [99]. The actuator can respond
to light in a self-regulating way, similar to how
plants adjust their position towards sunlight.
By adjusting a baffle plate’s position relative
to the incident light beam, positive feedback
can be programmed, enabling the transforma-
tion of a light-bending strip into a morphing
switcher [99].
(2) PI and PID control
Negative feedback is common in biological
systems for maintaining a control parameter.
For example, homeostasis can be modelled as
a proportional-integral feedback system [ 100].
This control technique is ideal for systems
that are not highly dynamic. An example of
a dynamic system that requires fine control
is bipedal standing. Bipedal standing can be
modelled as a proportional-integral-derivative
feedback system [ 101]. Standing balance in
humanoid robots often involves PID control
[102].
(3) Antagonistic muscle pairs
In general, muscles work in antagonistic pairs,
such as flexion and extension muscles. Having
antagonistic muscle pairs makes it possible for
two opposing muscles to keep a joint in resid-
ual tension, also known as muscle tone. Residual
tension has a number of purposes: (i) helps pos-
ture by reducing passive stretching; (ii) speeds
up muscle response to instructions to move;
and (iii) removes laxity and backlash in joints.
These things improve the control performance
of the joints. Examples of robot joints that utilise
antagonistic actuators are given in [ 103, 104].
(4) Fine force resolution
Human muscle is capable of fine force resolu-
tion. For example, human fingers can apply a
force with the index finger of as little as 10 mN
[105] which is over three orders of magnitude
below the maximum force capability [106]. One
reason for this fine resolution is that individual
muscles are comprised of many individual mus-
cle units and these muscle units can be recruited
gradually by the nervous system. The nervous
system is also able to recruit smaller units first
in order to create either a very small force or
a gradually increasing force [ 107]. This recruit-
ment strategy is called Henneman’s size princi-
ple which states that motor units are recruited in
order from smallest to largest based on the inten-
sity of the force being applied [ 107]. Small-to-
large recruitment minimises force fluctuations
and energy expenditure.
A bioinspired actuator assembly that grad-
ually recruits individual actuators has been
achieved using bundles of fluidic artificial
muscles [ 108]. The design uses a uniformly
applied pressure to many individual fluidic arti-
ficial muscles while creating differential thresh-
old pressures among the artificial muscles [108].
An ultrafast, miniature soft actuator has been
developed that can apply very small accurate
forces [109].
(5) Miniature sensors
The human body contains several million micro
sized sensors that enable sensor-rich feedback
information such as position, acceleration, pres-
sure and pain. Sensors are particularly densely

packed in the feet to aid locomotion and balance
control. And sensors are densely packed in the
hands to aid dexterity. Such a rich supply of sen-
sory data helps achieve robust control. A review
of bioinspired miniature flexible sensors is given
in [110].
(6) Multisensory integration
One of the challenges of having a vast diversity
and quantity of sensor feedback is how to deal
with that information quickly and effectively. In
locomotion, animals have multisensory integra-
tion that combines diverse types of feedback into
a form that can be processed very quickly [ 111,
112]. Examples of bioinspired sensory integra-
tion include [113, 114].
(7) Local reflexes
Local reflexes represent an automatic localised
actuation response to certain stimuli such as a
force or displacement and have the important
advantage of creating a fast response. A clas-
sic example of a reflex is the extension of the
knee which is caused by stretching the mus-
cle spindle in the quadriceps muscle. The reflex
is a monosynaptic reflex, meaning it involves a
direct connection between the sensory nerve, the
spinal cord, and the motor nerve, thus avoid-
ing data travelling to and from the brain. The
reflex contributes to stability in standing and
locomotion in humans and is therefore of inter-
est to robotics engineers [ 115]. When standing
upright, the quadriceps muscles work to keep the
knees extended and prevent the body from col-
lapsing. Other examples of a local reflex include
the withdrawal reflex (nociceptive flexion reflex)
of the hand from hot surfaces and, as mentioned
in section 3, the palmer grasp reflex of new-
born babies. A biologically inspired controller
based on reflex circuits has recently been devel-
oped for robots [ 116]. These reflex circuits self-
organise via Hebbian learning driven by spon-
taneous motor activity. The circuits are inte-
grated, creating a computationally inexpensive
task-space controller [116].
(8) Muscle synergies
The human body has around 600 muscles
but only around 100 active joints and around
200 DoF . The result is that many joints have
redundant actuation with more muscles than
the mechanical DoF . This redundancy enables a
wide range of muscle activation patterns to be
produced for the same movement. Redundant
muscles give robustness because if a muscle is
injured or tired or misaligned, other muscle pat-
terns can be adopted to make movements.
Muscle synergies greatly simplify control
by causing movements with a single neural
command [ 117, 118]. The co-activation of
muscles is usually learned and organised in
a task-dependent manner. Controlling multi-
jointed limbs involves the challenging task of
controlling redundant DoF . The nervous system
of animals simplifies this control task by using
global variables such as end-points that have
fewer DoF . These end-points can be reduced to
simple parameters like a direction component
and a length component.
Actuator synergies for robots have been devel-
oped using techniques such as reinforcement
learning with reward-function algorithms
and trial and error. A bioinspired synergis-
tic actuation strategy has been developed
for a quadruped robot using four actuators
[119]. The synergy-based quadruped requires
minimal actuation and exhibits emergent,
animal-like dynamic responses, including pas-
sive robustness to environmental perturba-
tions and a wide range of actuated behaviours
[119]. A synergy-based actuation system
has been developed for a humanoid robot
inspired by the fascia network of the human
body [120].
(9) Hierarchical control
In a hierarchy, low level control mechanisms are
controlled by a higher-level control mechanism,
with a single controller ultimately in charge
[121]. Hierarchical control can be efficient
because a locally controlled event can be con-
trolled faster and more accurately. Hierarchical
control can also be more robust because if a local
area is damaged or lost then the whole system is
not necessarily affected.
The musculoskeletal nervous system of ani-
mals adopts a hierarchical control strategy which
can be described in three levels [ 122]. At the
highest level is the cerebral cortex where the
strategy for movement takes place. At the inter-
mediate level are the cortical motor areas and
cerebellum which determine the sequences of
muscle contractions required to achieve the
high-level goal. At the lowest level are the brain
stem and spinal cord which activate the motor
neurons that generate the goal-directed move-
ments. A further layer of complexity in biol-
ogy is that individual cells are also systems that
receive signals, encode, and process informa-
tion, and respond, for example, by manufactur-
ing proteins [123].
An electronic architecture based on the
human motor system has been developed for
rehabilitation exoskeletons [ 124]. The archi-
tecture is divided into three hierarchical lev-
els: perception-intention, pattern generator,
and execution, facilitating modularity, scal-
ability, and parallelism in the execution and
operation of the system [ 124]. A bioinspired

learning-based hierarchical control system has
been developed for legged robotic locomotion
[125]. T wo distinct neural networks were used:
one tasked with modulating the frequency and

to generate the basic locomotor rhythm and
the other responsible for receiving environ-
mental perception data and directly modulat-

to execute precise movements on challenging
terrains [125].
(10) Adaptive control
Adaptive control can be defined as a feedback
control system that uses intelligence or algo-
rithms to adjust its characteristics in a chang-
ing environment in order to continually oper-
ate in an optimal manner. In contrast to tra-
ditional engineering systems, animals have rel-
atively robust control which is able to adapt to
significant changes in system dynamics. Animals
are able to detect changes in the control loop and
make adaptations based on experience as well as
trial and error [126, 127]. For example, if an ani-
mal loses a limb or an appendage, it is usually
able to learn how to control a new way of mov-
ing despite the major changes in the structure of
the system [127].
Adaptive control techniques such as self-tuning
PID are a well-established technique in areas such as
aircraft control where account is taken of changes in
parameters such as fuel mass distribution. However,
such adaptive control usually requires knowledge of
the system characteristics and may not be able to
adapt to significant changes to the system dynam-
ics. Modern bioinspired techniques in adaptive con-
trol, however, seek to detect changes in the control
loop and make adjustments using techniques such as
pattern generation, learning, fuzzy logic and neural
networks. The use of learning and CPGs is becom-
ing a common approach to adaptive control of robot
locomotion [128].
6.Discussion
This paper has identified 14 functionally different
types of moveable joints in humans, 10 of which have
more than one degree of freedom. The single degree
of freedom joints are the hinge joint, the pivot joint,
the planar joint and the fibula gliding joint. In con-
trast, humanoid robots typically have just three or
four types of joint, with only one having more than
one degree of freedom such as the MCP knuckle joint.
The wider range of joints, and greater DoF in human
joints allows for a higher level of fine-tuning of design
and more complex movements.

Joints 6–9 in table 2 affect arm and hand dex-

6.2.Designstrategiesforfuturehumanoidrobotics
As well as drawing inspiration from the design of
human joints, there is also potential for copying some
of the design strategies that can be seen in the design
of the musculoskeletal system as shown in table 3.
These strategies are generally different to traditional
engineering design approaches and can inspire a dif-
ferent and more effective approach to robot design.
(1) Multi-DoF joints
Multi-DoF joints lead to a compact design with
high functionality, as demonstrated with joints
in the wrist and ankle. A compact design leads
to a host of benefits such as low mass, low rota-
tional inertia and low frontal area. Joint freedom
is achieved in two main ways in human joints:
firstly, through curved joints—either spherical,
ellipsoidal or saddle; and secondly, by some kind
of suspension as with the jaw joint, knee joint
and hyoid bone. The development of multi-
DoF joints is a key goal for improving robot
performance [ 129]. There has been progress
in producing multi-DoF joints, although these
are mainly at the level of research prototypes
[129, 130].
(2) Segmented joints
Segmented joints give the opportunity to create
structures that perform special mechanical func-
tions such as combining strength and flexibility
in the spinal column and creating a morphing
pad in the toes. In both these cases, the stiffness

(3) Linkage joints
Joints with linkage mechanisms give the possibil-
ity for fine-tuning complex motion paths, as with
the wrist pronation joint and knee joint. Linkage
joints are very common in fish jaws, snake jaws
and insect wing joints which indicates they are
good for fine-tuning design goals such as speed
amplification and force amplification as well as
motion paths [ 132]. Therefore, linkage systems
should be considered for robotic joints.
(4) Soft structures
As described in sections 4.2 and 5.1, the human
musculoskeletal system has built-in compliance
in soft tissue (ligaments, tendons and mus-
cles) which makes joints tolerant of impacts and
enforced displacements. Another advantage of
soft design is that it is often inherently more
mass efficient. The reason is that stiffness design
involves meeting a displacement goal that often

involves low stress levels. In contrast, strength
design allows a structure to be designed to expe-
rience the maximum possible level of stress, thus
making more efficient use of material [ 133].
The difference between stiffness design and
strength design can be illustrated with the design
of a rectangular cantilever beam of Y oung’s mod-
ulus E, length L, depth d and variable width b.
Using simple beam theory and assuming small
deflections, the deflection, δ, of this beam for a
given load, P ,is approximately given by:
δ = 4PL3
Ebd3 . (1)
For a given load, P, the mass, m, of the rectangu-
lar beam is therefore inversely proportional to the
deflection as follows:
m ∝ b ∝ 1δ . (2)
T o minimise mass, the deflection has to be max-
imised up to the maximum allowable stress level.
In practice, when a beam is designed for stiff-
ness, the deflections are relatively very small com-
pared to strength design and hence a beam that
is designed for stiffness can weigh several times
that of a beam designed for strength [ 133]. One
challenge of large deflections is that displacements
are non-linear and can be more difficult to con-
trol. The current trend to develop technologies for
soft robotics has potential to help make signifi-
cant progress in reducing the mass of humanoid
robots.
(5) Multifunctioning parts
Multifunctioning is seen not just in joints but also
in muscles, ligaments and tendons. The advantage
of multifunctioning is that it reduces the num-
ber of parts which has the knock-on advantage
of reducing the number of interfaces, increasing
compactness and lowering weight. As shown in
section 5.1, muscles are remarkably multifunc-
tioning. They perform the roles of (i) actuator;
(ii) giving optimal shape to the body; (iii) forming
protective padding at optimal places (e.g. thenar
pad in the hands); (iv) giving a heat source for the
blood; (v) providing energy storage and release
during locomotion; and (vi) giving position feed-
back. T raditional engineering actuators have not
been multifunctional but focused on actuation.
However, there is now progress in producing mul-
tifunctioning actuators for robots [ 134–136].
(6) Redundant actuation
Human joints have redundancy in each aspect of
actuation. There are redundant DOF in joints,
redundant actuators and redundant neurophys-
iological DOF with multiple motoneurons on
the same muscle. As explained in section 5.2,
redundant actuation has the advantage of giv-
ing a wide range of muscle activation patterns
for the same movement. And redundant mus-
cles produce robustness because if one muscle
is injured or tired, other muscle patterns can
be adopted to make movements. But there is
a challenge with such redundancy in that con-
trol is open-ended with no single optimal way
of moving joints. This approach is very differ-
ent to traditional engineering where the goal is
generally to pursue exact constraint design where
each degree of freedom has one actuator. Most
current humanoid robots follow that exact con-
straint design approach. However, there are now
studies on how to co-ordinate redundant DoF in
humanoid robots [137].
(7) High integration
A major difference between humans and robots is
the level of integration. The human body has very
high levels of integration between the muscu-
loskeletal system, nervous system and blood cir-
culation system. Each limb contains a few dozen
muscles with many muscles containing over a
hundred motor units. This means there are thou-
sands of motor units in each limb. The ner-
vous system in each limb must have thousands
of nerve pathways integrated into the limb mus-
cles. Additional elements that have to be inte-
grated are sensors and blood supply. The fact that
biological systems are assembled, maintained and
repaired from the bottom up makes deep integra-
tion possible. T echnologies like additive manufac-
ture are making it easier to achieve higher levels of
integration [4].
(8) Advanced control
Processing power and complex control does not
cost much in terms of space and mass because
the human brain is so efficient from a compu-
tational point of view. The computational per-
formance (flops per kg per watt) of the human
brain is several orders of magnitude greater than
the best digital computers [ 138]. Therefore, it
makes sense that the human musculoskeletal sys-
tem makes much use of complex control methods
that require high processing power.
6.3.Theoriginofdesignsophisticationin
biologicalsystems
The human musculoskeletal system has a level of
design sophistication far higher than the best cur-
rent humanoid robots [ 1–3, 139]. A recent research
paper on finger biomechanics states the following: ‘In
the orchestra of human movement, the fingers of the
hand perform a symphony of astonishing complexity,
orchestrated by biomechanical engineering of preci-
sion’ [59]. One textbook on kinesiology states that the
structures of the foot ‘work in perfect synchronisa-
tion’ [140].

The extreme levels of sophistication in hands and
feet are surprising given that evolutionary changes
are assumed to be highly constrained to small step
changes from one generation to the next. Steven V ogel
has explained this apparent contradiction this way:
‘The dazzling diversity of the living world too easily
disguises the fact that the evolutionary process faces
constraints far more severe than anything impeding
human designers. ’ [141]. Some studies on complex
biological systems point to the non-incremental ori-
gin of design concepts. In a review of fish jaw link-
age mechanisms, W esneat stated that some of these
mechanisms have the appearance of sudden ‘transi-
tions in engineering design’ [142]. In a detailed review
of mechanical linkage mechanisms in nature, Muller
stated that it is very difficult to postulate how com-
plex linkage mechanisms could evolve in small steps
[143]. In research on proteins, Miller has shown that
many proteins have such unique structures that they
could not have appeared by small step changes [144].
One theory for explaining the origin of novel con-
cepts in biological systems without relying on incre-
mental change is that of co-option and exaptation.
This involves existing genes and structures becom-
ing useful for different future functions [ 145, 146].
This can potentially explain how an organism can
suddenly carry out a complex function that is new to
that organism. However, co-option does not explain
how each original concept first appeared. Another
theory for explaining sudden innovations in nature
is to rely on changes to Hox genes that have a role in
specifying body plans and patterns [ 147]. However,
body plans involve a detailed specification of many
parameters and to change from one viable body plan
to another viable body plan would require many
simultaneous changes [ 148]. The extreme levels of
design sophistication in nature have led some biolo-
gists to argue for a review of the classical evolutionary
assumption of incremental step changes [149, 150]. In
addition, some scientists have argued that intelligent
design concepts such as goal-directed design are nec-
essary to explain the complexity of biological systems
[151–156].
7.Conclusions
Current humanoid robots are far behind humans
in terms of locomotion agility and hand dexterity.
One of the key reasons is that the human muscu-
loskeletal system has many types of joint with multi-
ple DoF . This paper has described 14 different types of
moveable joint in humans with 10 having more than
one degree of freedom. Multiple DoF are produced
through sophisticated design features such as linkage
mechanisms, suspension systems and segmentation.
A key reason for the lack of agility in humanoid robots
is the use of stiff flat feet. T o improve locomotion
agility, a key goal for future humanoid robots is to
have flexible feet with actuated MTP joints. A key rea-
son for the lack of dexterity in robot hands is the lim-
ited DoF of the thumb and the inability to produce
the push-retract motion. T o improve hand dexterity,
key future goals include replicating the tendon path-
ways of human fingers and replicating the compact
linkage pronation mechanism of the human wrist.
Human joints contain design strategies that are dif-
ferent to traditional engineering such as high DoF ,
redundant actuation and multisensory integration.
These strategies have potential to inspire a different
and more effective approach to the mechanical design
of humanoid robots.
