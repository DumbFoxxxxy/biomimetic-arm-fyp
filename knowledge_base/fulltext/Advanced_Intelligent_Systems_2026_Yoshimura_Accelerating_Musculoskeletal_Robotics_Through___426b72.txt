RESEARCH ARTICLE 
Accelerating Musculoskeletal Robotics Through 
Parametric Design and 3D‐Printed Flexible Structures
Shunnosuke Yoshimura 
 | Kento Kawaharazuka 
 | Kei Okada
Graduate School of Information Science and Technology, The University of Tokyo, Tokyo, Japan
Correspondence: Shunnosuke Yoshimura ( yoshimura@jsk.imi.i.u-tokyo.ac.jp )
Received: 27 May 2026 | Revised: 24 July 2026 | Accepted: 18 August 2026
Keywords: 3D printing | biomimetic robots | flexible structures | musculoskeletal robotics | parametric design
ABSTRACT
The human body moves smoothly through coordinated soft elements including muscles, tendons, ligaments, and cushioning 
tissues. Conventional musculoskeletal robots often simplify these using wires or rigid joints, limiting biological flexibility and 
requiring extensive customization. Thus, a unified framework for designing biomimetic compliant elements remains lacking. 
Here, parametric design is integrated with flexible 3D printing to generate muscle‐, tendon‐, ligament‐, and cushioning‐inspired 
compliant components through a unified fabrication process using a single thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) material. Tuning 
pattern parameters, like lattice density and branch thickness, tailors mechanical properties across body regions. Additionally, 
ligament‐based joint constraints and integrated tendon‐driven actuation enable continuous, coordinated multijoint leg motions 
at life scale under external suspension, with partial body‐weight support and torso roll, pitch, and yaw each constrained from 
−20° to +20°. These results validate patterned flexible structures as a practical framework for musculoskeletal robot construction, 
simplifying design‐to‐fabrication workflows and enabling scalable biomimetic systems with potential extensions to whole‐body 
humanoids and embedded sensing.
1 | Introduction
Human and animal bodies are composed of diverse soft 
elements such as muscles, tendons, ligaments, and soft tissues, 
which cooperate to generate complex and smooth motions. 
Understanding these mechanisms of soft bodies and reproducing 
them in robots is an important challenge directly linked to 
elucidating motor science and acquiring advanced robotic func­
tions. Musculoskeletal robots mimic the biological driving prin­
ciple in which muscles pull bones to produce body motion 
and reconstruct it in robotic form, thereby contributing to the 
understanding of locomotor mechanisms and the reproduction of 
bodily functions.
Since biological bodies are composed of diverse soft elements 
whose properties and distributions vary substantially across body 
regions, their unified reproduction remains difficult. In previous 
studies, musculoskeletal robots have been realized by simplifying 
the skeleton and joints and by substituting muscles and ligaments 
with wires or rigid links. However, these nonbiological structures 
differ greatly from the shapes, materials, and functional princi­
ples of biological soft elements, limiting the reproduction of the 
inherent flexibility and multifunctionality of muscles, tendons, 
and ligaments.
Robots that mimic the complex skeletal structures and tendon‐
driven mechanisms characteristic of biological systems have been 
developed, such as Kenta [1], which incorporated such mecha‐
nisms, and Kotaro [2], a child‐sized robot that achieved biologically 
inspired complex mechanisms including a shoulder [3] and whole‐
body motions [4]. Kojiro [5] and Kenzoh [6] achieved powerful 
movements through enhanced motor output. The ECCEROBOT 
family [7] incorporated muscle–tendon complexes, while Roboy 
[8], though child‐sized, introduced interaction into tendon‐driven

Although these robots reproduced the overall muscle arrangement 
and the number of muscles with high fidelity, the heavy reliance 
on substitute structures such as wires lacking muscular volume 
and simple rotational joints instead of ligament‐based joints limited 
their ability to reproduce biological mechanical properties and the 
interactions among flexible components.
To address this problem, attempts have been made to employ 
soft materials and emerging actuators. Pneumatic muscle actua­
tors [17–19], origami‐based structures [20, 21], HASEL actuators 
with self‐healing capability [ 22–24], and thread‐based actuators 
[25–28] have been proposed. However, these alternative elements 
have not been designed under a common design principle, leaving 
issues of generality and applicability in the design process. 
Moreover, they often relied on multimaterial use and manual 
fabrication processes, leading to complexity and inefficiency in 
production. As a result, current musculoskeletal robots are largely 
limited to wire‐driven structures powered by motors or musculos­
keletal architectures employing pneumatic artificial muscles such 
as McKibben‐type actuators [29–34].
Meanwhile, recent advances in 3D printing technologies have 
enabled the inexpensive and rapid fabrication of flexible 
and complexly shaped body elements. Research has explored 
endowing mechanical properties through organic geometries 
[35–37], and fabricating body parts via 3D printing [ 38–40], 
bringing such techniques into wider and more versatile use 
[41–43]. Furthermore, parametric design, which has developed 
in the field of architecture [ 44, 45], can automatically generate 
complex structures solely through parameter settings, and its 
application to engineering design is also anticipated [ 46, 47]. 
Therefore, combining 3D printing with parametric design makes 
it possible to achieve the unified and efficient design and fabrica­
tion of flexible body elements, which was previously difficult.
Recent studies have demonstrated musculoskeletal and locomo­
tion systems based on architected materials. Guan et al. [ 48] 
blended and superimposed multiple lattice topologies within 
a single elastic material, producing continuously varying soft‐
to‐rigid structures and a tendon‐driven elephant robot capable 
of trunk manipulation and walking. Kim et al. [ 49] used motor‐
driven TPU handed‐shearing auxetic structures directly as load‐
bearing legs and achieved untethered quadrupedal walking at 
0.65 body lengths per second. Related actuators combining TPU 
auxetic structures and origami bellows generated up to 75 N and 
were demonstrated as artificial muscles in a battery‐powered 
human‐scale single leg [ 50]. These studies establish important 
benchmarks in spatial stiffness programming, untethered loco­
motion, and individual actuator performance.
However, despite recent advances, three challenges remain:
• Biological flexibility and multifunctionality remain difficult 
to reproduce due to reliance on nonbiological substitute 
structures for body parts.
• Each body part often requires individual design, lacking a 
common design principle.
• Fabrication can require multiple materials and skilled 
manual work, constraining efficiency and generality.
In this study, we propose a unified design‐and‐fabrication frame­
work for musculoskeletal soft elements that integrates parametric 
design with 3D printing. Here, “unified” refers to a common work­
flow that converts prescribed anatomical volumes and surfaces 
into parameterized 3D lattices or 2D surface patterns, adjusts their 
mechanical properties according to functional requirements, 
fabricates them using a single TPU material and FDM process, 
and integrates them into a musculoskeletal system. Mechanical 
properties can be adjusted through structural parameters such as 
lattice density and strut diameter.
For brevity, these components are hereafter referred to as muscles, 
tendons, ligaments, and cushioning elements according to their 
intended functional roles in the musculoskeletal robot. This 
terminology does not imply complete reproduction of the struc­
tures, material properties, or underlying physical mechanisms of 
the corresponding biological tissues.
Compared with the recent lattice‐based robotic systems reported 
by Guan et al. [48] and Kim et al. [49, 50], the main contribution 
of this work is a pattern‐structured robotic framework that adapts 
lattice‐based designs to diverse prescribed shapes and functional 
roles, and integrates them into an anatomically organized, life‐
sized human lower‐limb system. Guan et al. [ 48] and Kim et al. 
[49] used lattice structures as the principal load‐bearing body or 
leg architecture, spanning roles otherwise assigned to bones and 
joints. In contrast, our approach preserves the anatomical organ­
ization of a rigid skeletal framework surrounded by soft muscles 
and ligaments that provide actuation and joint constraints. The 
high‐output actuator developed by Kim et al. [ 50] focuses on 
individual artificial‐muscle performance, whereas our study 
emphasizes geometric adaptability and the integration of multiple 
muscles and soft functional elements across multiple joints.
The specific contributions of this study are as follows: (i) tuning 
anisotropic stiffness through asymmetric cell dimensions within 
the same lattice topology, thereby enabling functional differentia­
tion without combining different cell types; (ii) generating struc­
tural gradients from compliant muscle‐inspired regions to stiff 
tendon‐like regions within a single TPU print, thereby reducing 
part‐specific fabrication and manual assembly; (iii) constructing 
ligament‐inspired joint constraints by projecting 2D patterns 
onto 3D joint surfaces, thereby defining joint ranges of motion 
without relying on rigid mechanisms or bulk lattice deformation; 
and (iv) applying the common parametric design principle to 
integrate muscle‐, tendon‐, ligament‐, and cushioning‐inspired 
components into a life‐sized leg system, rather than limiting the 
application to an individual element or a single joint.
We found that diverse soft elements with distinct functions can be 
reproduced even when fabricated via 3D printing using a single 
thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) material, thereby simplifying 
the integrated workflow from design to fabrication. Additionally, 
we evaluated these flexible elements, including their long‐term 
characteristics, and clarified practical considerations for their

2 | Results
In this study, we propose an integrated design and fabrication 
framework that fuses parametric design with fused deposition 
modeling (FDM) 3D printing to generate musculoskeletal‐
inspired soft elements through a shared parameterized workflow 
using a single TPU material. In this section, we first outline the 
concept and generation method of the Flexible Pattern Structure, 
and present element‐level applications corresponding to muscles, 
tendons, ligaments, and cushioning elements. Furthermore, we 
quantitatively evaluate the mechanical properties of these pattern 
structures, and verify their effectiveness through integration into 
a ligament‐constrained joint and a life‐sized musculoskeletal 
leg robot. Finally, we demonstrate continuous, coordinated multi­
joint leg motions under partial body‐weight support conditions, 
serving as a demonstration of the integrated musculoskeletal 
system. These results support the viability of flexible pattern struc­
tures as a generalized framework that enables the construction 
of integrated musculoskeletal robots while reducing reliance on 
nonbiological substitute mechanisms and the use of multiple 
materials. An overview of the proposed method is shown in 
Figure 1.
2.1 | Overview of Flexible Pattern Structure
In this study, we introduce the Flexible Pattern Structure as 
compliant elements inspired by musculoskeletal soft tissues. 
These are fabricated as 3D lattice structures and 2D surface 
pattern structures via FDM 3D printing using TPU95A (Figure 2).
The 3D lattice structure is composed based on a unit element that 
connects the vertices of a rectangular cuboid with branch‐like 
struts (Figure 2A1). These unit elements are repeatedly arranged 
at equal intervals or with a spatial gradient in the x, y, and 
z directions. By adjusting the strut diameter p, lattice spacing 
(x, y, z), and branch structure, mechanical properties such as 
compressive stiffness and deformation range can be controlled. 
The design of these parameters is detailed in a subsequent section. 
Additionally, because the struts mutually support each other 
during layer‐by‐layer fabrication, they can be printed without 
internal support materials.
On the other hand, the 2D pattern structure is created by projec­
ting a branch‐like network generated on a 2D plane onto a curved 
surface (Figure 2B). This method can provide ligament‐like joint 
constraints with small restoring torques while maintaining a wide 
range of motion against twisting and bending. Although the 2D 
pattern requires support material during printing, its removal 
is relatively easy because the structure is arranged on the outer 
surface (Figure 2C).
Through these pattern structures, compliant elements inspired 
by muscles, tendons, ligaments, and cushioning elements can be 
integrally generated while adjusting their mechanical properties 
under a unified parametric design principle using a single TPU 
material. In this study, we adopt a step‐by‐step procedure: we first 
prototype at the unit‐structure level and experimentally evaluate 
the mechanical properties corresponding to desired functions 
such as compression, tension, and joint constraint. Subsequently, 
we reflect the obtained parameters in the part‐scale design to 
fabricate the entire body part. This stepwise flow enables efficient 
fabrication while minimizing the number of prototyping itera­
tions associated with design changes at the part scale. Collectively, 
these approaches provide a design method for integrating soft 
elements into the internal structures of biomimetic robots.
2.2 | Mechanical Characterization
We evaluated the mechanical properties of the flexible pattern 
structures (muscle‐ and tendon‐type lattices) in terms of velocity‐
dependent hysteresis (A–E), cyclic loading or fatigue (F–H), and 
creep and recovery under constant load (I, J). Two conditions, 
Conditions A and B, were established by changing the printing 
orientation. Additionally, we evaluated the ligament constraint 
properties of a hip joint as a representative joint (K, L). The 
data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3 independent specimens). 
Three specimens per condition were used to evaluate specimen‐
to‐specimen variation while covering multiple loading and time‐
dependent conditions. The tests were intended to identify engi­
neering trends for design selection and repeated robotic oper­
ation, rather than to establish a constitutive material model. 
Because the simplified specimens were designed to isolate the 
mechanical behavior of a regular lattice, their measured proper­
ties may differ from those of the integrated muscle components. 
In the integrated components, the lattice‐cell geometry varies 
spatially to conform to the complex 3D muscle shapes, and the 
structures additionally contain internal wire‐routing channels 
and external protective patterned shells. The main trends are 
summarized in Figure 3.

Geometric parameters were selected by setting candidate ranges 
according to required functions and experimentally mapping

Because the flexible and sparse lattice patterns addressed in this 
study undergo large deformations and are heavily influenced by 
the 3D printing process, we adopted a strategy of relying on empir­
ical mapping derived from actual measurements rather than 
theoretical predictions. The parameters are defined as follows: 
for muscles, x represents the compression direction from external 
forces, y is the contraction direction (governing softness and range 
of motion), z is the unit cell dimension in the nonload‐bearing 
direction, and p is the strut diameter (affecting overall stiffness 
and printability). For tendons, while the structure is similar, the 
primary load is tension rather than compression. We determined 
these parameters systematically in the following steps.
First, we determined p, which governs overall stiffness and print­
ability. Increasing p from 1.5 to 3 mm exponentially increases 
Young’s modulus by approximately 28 times. We set p = 2 mm as 
the baseline, representing the minimum dimension that balances 
printability and flexibility.
Second, we adjusted x and y. While setting both to 5 mm yields 
a high stiffness of 0.08–0.27 MPa, values around 15 mm ensure 
stable flexibility (0.005–0.01 MPa) and provide a contact strain 
exceeding 0.5, yielding a sufficient range of motion.
Third, we finely tuned the overall properties using z. By increasing 
z from 25 to 40 mm, the stiffness can be mildly reduced to approx­
imately 1/2 to 1/5. Through this design procedure, assigning 
parameters such as ( x, y, z, p) = (15, 10, 20, 2) [mm] achieves a 
Young’s modulus of ∼0.02 MPa in the x‐direction and ∼0.01 MPa 
in the y‐direction, thereby differentiating muscle flexibility 
according to required functions using a single material.
For tendon elements, x was reduced to achieve high stiffness. 
Based on tensile tests of isotropic structures, we designated x as 
the dominant parameter for stiffness. In tensile tests with x = {5, 6, 
7, 8} [mm] (Figure 4D), Young’s modulus increased roughly line­
arly from 1.07 to 2.61 MPa as x decreased.
Examples of generation are shown in Figure 4C. In the muscle‐
tendon complex (C1), the low‐density side was designed as a 
highly deformable muscle, while the high‐density side acted as 
a highly stiff tendon (series elastic element). As mechanical refer­
ence values, the muscle element exhibited a compressive stiffness 
of 1.3 N/mm, while the tendon element exhibited a tensile stiff­
ness of 15 N/mm. While these values do not indicate strict equiva­
lence with actual biological tissues, they serve as design guidelines 
reflecting the macroscopic deformation behaviors reported in 
the literature for muscles (maximum contraction of ∼20%) and 
tendons (10–100 N/mm, strain of ∼10%) [52, 53]. Furthermore, 
regarding specific parameter settings, relatively high‐density 
patterns (x, y, z  ≈ 12, 12, 25 mm) were used for internal muscles 
that operate without losing shape even under friction against 
bones. Conversely, lower‐density patterns ( x, y ≈ 12.5, 15, and 
z = 30 mm) were used for external muscles that undergo large 
deformations and act as cushions.
Finally, in the sole element (C2), support was provided through 
branch orientation and a thickness gradient, demonstrating 
threshold stiffening that increased from ∼5 N/mm during the 
initial 5 mm to ∼30 N/mm thereafter with a 15 mm indenter. 
From the above, we demonstrated that structural design and 
functional differentiation tailored to required functions can be 
achieved through the control of pattern parameters using a single 
TPU material.
2.4 | Joint and Ligament
To mimic biological joints constrained by ligaments, we propose 
a method for designing ligament arrangements by projecting 2D 
patterns onto a cylindrical surface (Figure 5A1,A2).
By unfolding the target cylindrical surface into a 2D plane, 
arranging patterns with different orientations and densities, 
and projecting them back, different joint constraint directions 
can be specified. In this study, these patterns were designed 
as functional layouts for providing joint constraints and ranges 
of motion, rather than for accurately reproducing a prescribed 
morphed shape. A 1‐DOF rotational joint can be constructed 
using directionally oriented patterns, while a multi‐DOF joint 
can use an isotropic lattice. Furthermore, overlapping multiple 
projection planes can represent complex constraints, such as the 
cruciate ligaments of the knee (Figure 5C). Examples of diverse 
ligamentous joints generated from the same input surface are 
shown in Figure 5B.
Using this method, we prototyped a hip joint (ball joint), a knee 
joint (1‐DOF hinge), and an ankle joint (flexion‐extension hinge) 
(Figure 5C). The ranges of motion (ROM) were as follows: hip 
joint (flexion 90 °/extension 30/abduction 60 °/adduction 30 °), 
knee joint (flexion 150°), and ankle joint (dorsiflexion 30 °/plan­
tarflexion 15°). These are comparable to representative values for 
typical human joints (hip: 120 °/20°/40°/25°, knee: 140 °, ankle: 
20°/60°) [54].
To validate the practicality of these bearing‐free joints, we evalu­
ated fatigue under repetitive motion and stress relaxation under 
static loading. The data are presented as mean  ± SD (n = 3 inde­
pendent specimens). Measuring joint torque during repetitive 
motion revealed that the majority of the initial torque was main­
tained after 999 cycles (e.g., ∼87% at 90° hip flexion, 79% at 135° 
knee flexion, and 94% at 30° ankle dorsiflexion), confirming that 
the joints function without constraint failure.
For stress relaxation and recovery, the joints were fixed for 3 h 
at hip flexion 90 °, knee flexion 180 °, or ankle dorsiflexion 30 °, 
and their torque–angle responses were measured at 0, 10, 30, 
and 60 min after release ( n = 3, mean ± SD). Although an initial 
torque decrease due to stress relaxation was observed immediately 
after release, viscoelastic behavior confirmed that the original 
constraint force recovered over time. The prototype and detailed 
data are provided in Figures S14, S37 and S38, together with Tables 
S18 and S19.

3 | Discussion and Conclusion
In this study, by integrating parametric design and 3D printing 
of TPU, we proposed a framework to design, generate, and 
integrate multiple soft elements inspired by muscles, tendons, 
ligaments, and cushioning elements as a common “flexible 
pattern structure.”
Here, the contribution does not lie in the individual use of 
Rhino/Grasshopper or FDM printing but in organizing these 
existing tools into a common design‐to‐fabrication workflow 
for multiple functionally distinct musculoskeletal elements and 
integrating the resulting elements into an anatomically organized, 
life‐sized system.
Through this framework, anisotropic stiffness tuning, muscle–
tendon gradients, surface‐projected ligament patterns, and 
system‐level integration into a life‐sized leg were realized within 
a common single‐material design and fabrication process.
At the element level, the results demonstrated that functional 
differentiation can be achieved through parameter differences 
within the same lattice topology: muscle elements act as highly

These findings support the potential for an integrated design that 
mitigates the fragmentation of materials and structures for each 
element—a common issue in conventional approaches—and 
provides a design space for achieving both biomimetic structures

The 2D ligament patterns used in this study were selected to 
realize functional joint constraints rather than accurate target‐
shape morphing. The sensitivity of the constraint behavior to 
pattern resolution and geometry was not systematically evaluated. 
Finer patterns and sensitivity analysis will therefore be required 
for applications that require precise matching to a prescribed 
morphed shape.
For the 3D patterned structures, stiffness can be adjusted through 
two complementary strategies: broad‐range adjustment by collec­
tively changing the cell dimensions and fine adjustment by 
varying an individual geometric parameter. For broad‐range 
adjustment, x, y, and z are collectively changed while the branch 
thickness p is fixed. As shown in Figure 1, structures with cell 
dimensions of approximately 15 mm exhibited low stiffness over 
a broad compressive strain range and initial effective Young’s 
moduli below 0.3 MPa, whereas those with cell dimensions 
of approximately 7 mm exhibited effective Young’s moduli of 
approximately 1–2.5 MPa and a more nearly linear stress–strain 
response within the measured range. For fine adjustment, one of 
the four primary geometric parameters, x, y, z, and p, is varied 
while the other three are fixed. Within the parameter ranges 
used for the muscle‐inspired components ( x, y = 10–20 mm, 
z = 20–40 mm, and p = 1–3 mm), individual parameter variations 
produced changes in the effective Young’s modulus on the order 
of 10−3–10−2 MPa. Thus, collective changes in the cell dimensions 
can be used to select the overall stiffness characteristics, whereas 
individual parameter changes enable finer adjustment according 
to local functional requirements. In general, increasing x, y, or 
z, or decreasing p, reduced the effective Young’s modulus and 
increased structural compliance.
The conformal morphing metacylinder study [55] differs from this 
study in its purpose and deformation mechanism. Its apparent 
secant moduli, estimated at 10 mm displacement, were approxi­
mately 0.0009–0.013 MPa, whereas our initial effective Young’s 
moduli ranged from below 0.3 to approximately 2.5 MPa. Our 
method therefore covers a broader stiffness range, while the meta­
cylinder enables discrete tuning by combining multiple cylinders. 
Because of the different geometries and loading conditions, 
these values provide only an order‐of‐magnitude comparison. 
In addition, the reduced‐order modeling and physics‐enhanced 
neural‐network approach proposed by Mohammadi et al. [ 56] 
may provide a future means of predicting and inversely designing 
nonlinear stiffness responses from lattice parameters.
Within the tested conditions, all three specimens per condition 
completed the repetitive loading tests without rupture, and no 
visible interlayer separation was observed under large deforma­
tion. These results support the structural integrity of the printed 
structures within the evaluated loading and cycle ranges, while 
broader characterization of fatigue life and failure limits will 
require longer‐term testing with larger sample sets. For practical 
operation, the time‐ and cycle‐dependent behavior of TPU should 
be explicitly considered in system design and control, including 
creep, stress relaxation, and changes in mechanical response 
under cyclic loading. In the cyclic tests, changes in the measured 
mechanical properties were most prominent during the initial 
∼100 cycles and subsequently converged toward a stable response. 
Accordingly, preconditioning is recommended to reduce initial 
transient effects before deployment. Furthermore, because defor­
mations induced by creep and stress relaxation were partially 
reversible after unloading, their temporal recovery characteristics 
should be incorporated into the operational strategy.
In the integration experiments using a life‐sized lower limb 
model, we incrementally verified that passive constraints and 
active actuation can coexist. The single‐leg prototype reproduced 
representative joint motions through antagonistic and multijoint 
muscle combinations, with the ligamentous joints maintaining 
motion without dislocation. Furthermore, the bipedal robot 
exhibited anterior‐posterior stepping motions under external 
support, providing evidence for the viability of element integra­
tion in continuous coordinated motions at a life‐size scale. The 
external support was deliberately introduced to decouple balance 
control from structural evaluation, allowing the joint constraints 
and tendon‐driven actuation to be evaluated independently of 
whole‐body balance.
The proposed method addresses constraints and interferences 
as an integrated physical body from the perspective of design 
principles. This contrasts with the high routing fidelity of 
conventional wire‐driven musculoskeletal robots (Kenta [ 1], 
Kotaro [2–4], Kojiro [ 5], Kenzoh [ 6], ECCEROBOT family [ 7], 
Roboy [8], Kenshiro [9, 10], Kengoro [11–13], Musashi [14–16]). 
Conversely, increasing volumetric elements exacerbates imple­
mentation constraints like contact friction. In addition, unlike soft 
actuator systems (pneumatic muscles [17–19], origami structures 
[20, 21], HASEL actuators [22–24], fiber‐driven actuators [25–28]) 
that often focus on mimicking a single body part with complex 
processes, our unified design principle provides a straightforward 
and scalable fabrication pathway for integrating various soft 
elements into a life‐sized bipedal robot.
Regarding manufacturing trade‐offs, conventional molding tech­
niques require numerous manual processes and specialized skills. 
By exclusively using TPU 3D printing, our method eliminates 
mold preparation and complex post‐processing, keeping human 
labor time minimal. Conversely, the prolonged machine time 
required to print life‐sized soft parts (e.g., 20–40 h per muscle) 
is an unavoidable limitation. However, mapping characteristics 
from unit patterns minimizes whole‐body prototyping iterations. 
Consequently, the reduction in human intervention makes this a 
practical method that simplifies and facilitates overall soft robotic 
system development.
Another manufacturing constraint arises from the difference 
between volumetric 3D lattices and 2D patterns projected onto 
surfaces. Because internal support structures are difficult to 
access and remove after printing, the 3D lattices must be 
self‐supporting. This requirement restricts the available lattice 
topologies according to printability constraints such as over­
hang angles and bridging distances, thereby limiting geometric

Regarding sustainability, the 3D lattices used in this study require

the 3‐mm‐thick spring decreased to 52% after 25 cycles. Our 
structures cannot change stiffness during operation, but maintain 
their geometry‐defined properties without additional energy and 
showed no rupture within the tested 999 cycles.
Although this robot mimics the human musculoskeletal system, 
core differences from biological tissues exist at the element 
level. First, unlike human muscles where volumetric fibers 
actively generate contractile force, our actuation is handled by 
motors and wires, while the TPU lattice provides series elasticity 
and cushioning. Viewed as an integrated module, it engineers 
the dual nature of biological muscles: active contraction and 
impact cushioning. Second, regarding Poisson’s ratio, water‐rich 
human muscles bulge laterally under longitudinal contraction. 
In contrast, our sparse TPU lattice collapses internally, resulting 
in minimal lateral bulging. This nonbiological trait is advanta­
geous for suppressing unintended internal interference within 
the robot. Third, unlike biological tissues that continuously 
self‐repair via metabolism, the viscoelastic TPU elements inevi­
tably experience irreversible fatigue over long‐term use. While 
periodic maintenance is required, the mold‐free 3D printing 
approach allows rapid, low‐cost remanufacturing and replace­
ment of degraded parts.
This study has two main limitations. First, a generalized 
analytical design model linking lattice geometry to mechan‐
ical properties has not yet been established. Because flexible 
TPU lattices exhibit nonlinear responses, predicting mechanical 
properties directly from geometric parameters remains difficult. 
We utilized practical, empirical design rules requiring charac‐
teristic mapping via prototyping. The empirical design maps 
presented here are specific to TPU95A and the tested FDM 
conditions and are not intended as universal scaling laws across 
different materials or geometric scales. Although the dimen‐
sionless ratios p/x, y/x, and z/x provide a scale‐independent 
description of the idealized deformation geometry, they do not 
predict stress or stiffness across materials. Establishing such 
scaling laws would require systematic evaluation of normalized 
stiffness, relative density, and strut slenderness across multiple 
materials, scales, and printing conditions. Ideally, parameter 
determination methods based on physical models should be 
established. Additionally, achievable properties are restricted by 
material limits; for instance, achieving extremely high tendon 
stiffness requires structural enlargement.
Second, the prototype has not achieved walking without external 
support. The bipedal stepping demonstration relied on external 
suspension that provided partial body‐weight support and 
constrained torso roll, pitch, and yaw to  ±20°; autonomous 
walking was not demonstrated. The inherent flexibility and 
large deformations introduce kinematic variations (e.g., dynamic 
changes in moment arms and wire friction), complicating 
modeling. Unlike rigid components, soft structures lack invar­
iable dimensional stability, and viscoelastic variations make 
control challenging. Introducing machine learning approaches 
to acquire a body schema through actual operation is expected 
to compensate for these uncertainties and realize autono­
mous motions.
The proposed framework indicates broad potential for future 
applications. Parameter‐based design enables straightforward 
customization for different physiques. Future multimaterial 3D 
printing for monolithic fabrication of bones, muscles, and liga­
ments could advance design automation, extending applications 
to prosthetics, assistive exosuits, and sensor‐integrated artificial 
bodies. Future work will focus on whole‐body expansion and 
realizing environmental contact motions that leverage the passive 
adaptability of soft structures.
In conclusion, this study demonstrated that an integrated 
approach based on single‐material TPU and flexible pattern 
structures is applicable to life‐sized musculoskeletal systems. 
This provides a foundation for connecting the designs of 
soft elements—which have conventionally been fragmented 
in biomimetic robots—under a unified principle.
4 | Experimental Section
4.1 | Design Software
Parametric design was carried out using Rhinoceros 8 and 
Grasshopper as design software. The core component, which 
generates body elements composed of pattern structures from 
volume region input and pattern parameters, is defined as a 
Python script within Grasshopper. Rhinoceros, fundamentally a 
modeling software without a history function, allows real‐time 
confirmation of the model generated by changing parameters 
through integration with Grasshopper. This feature makes it 
possible to generate multiple models with different parame­
ters easily.

4.3 | Details of Ligament Design
This section details the formulation of a parametric design 
method for generating ligaments in design software. The cylin­
drical surfaces on which the ligaments are distributed are also 
represented by NURBS surfaces, which can handle continuous 
curves using a coordinate system defined on the surface. A 
separate flat NURBS surface is created, and the ligament pattern is 
defined on it. Although the shapes of these two surfaces differ, the 
commonality lies in the definition of positions on the surface in a 
2D surface coordinate system. Therefore, curves that serve as the 
basis for generating the ligament pattern structure are obtained 
by projecting the pattern on the flat surface onto the cylindrical 
surface according to the surface coordinate system. Finally, a 
printable pattern structure is generated by adding thickness, 
similar to muscles.
4.4 | 3D‐Printing Process
3D printing was performed using an FDM 3D printer. The mate­
rial for the flexible patterned structure is TPU95A [ 60]: thermo­
plastic polyurethane. The Young’s modulus is 9.8 ± 0.7 MPa in the 
transverse direction of the layers and 7.4  ± 0.6 MPa in the longi­
tudinal direction. The elongation at break is 650% in the trans­
verse direction and 480% in the longitudinal direction. Compared 
to more ordinary materials like PLA and ABS, this material 
exhibits rubber‐like properties and is deformable. In general, 
flexible materials for 3D printers have a hardness ranging from 
95 to 65 A. Among these, TPU95A offers high tensile strength and 
relatively low elongation but is easy to print. By leveraging these 
characteristics and combining them with patterned structures, 
it achieves both printability and flexibility. For fabrication, the 
Prusa XL [ 61], which utilizes a tool changer system to enable 
multimaterial printing including flexible materials, is employed. 
Printing details and parameters are listed in Table S2. The internal 
structure of the muscle (Figure 4A3‐1,A3‐2) does not require 
support during printing, but the muscle surface (Figure 4A3‐3) 
does. The ligaments also require supports, where tree supports 
are particularly effective. In multimaterial printers, switching the 
support material to PLA enables easy removal of the support 
after printing while achieving high‐quality prints. Even with 
conventional printers limited to one material, supports can be 
made using TPU, allowing for successful printing. The bones are 
printed using PLA and are fixed to muscles and ligaments using 
insert nuts and screws.
4.5 | Leg Hardware Configuration
The hardware configuration of the leg partially utilizes a system 
based on previous research [ 11, 14]. The overall system consists 
of motors and wire winding units, a PC, motor drivers, and power 
supplies for the motors and drivers. Maxon EC‐4pole motors [62] 
equipped with a 53:1 gearhead and an encoder and wire winding 
units developed in previous research [14] were used as actuators. 
In this setup, the PC was placed externally to the leg, sending 
commands via a LAN cable. Motor control was performed using 
encoders attached to the motors, with the wire length controlled 
as the target value. While the hardware configuration of the 
motors is fundamentally based on previous research, the proposed 
structural patterns for body elements created in this study are not 
dependent on the type or drive method of the motors. Therefore, 
as long as a mechanism for pulling wires can be implemented, 
the system can be easily adapted to other actuators, as well as 
pneumatic or hydraulic actuators.
