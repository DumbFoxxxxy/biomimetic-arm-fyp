Is an ellipsoid surface suitable to model the scapulothoracic sliding plane?

ABSTRACT 
Closed loop kinematic chain approaches are commonly used to assess scapular kinematics but with heteroge-
neous ellipsoid calibration procedures. This study aimed to assess whether an ellipsoid surface can model the 
scapulothoracic sliding plane and determine the optimal number of static poses to calibrate the ellipsoid pa-
rameters. An intracortical pin with a rigid cluster of four reflective markers was inserted into the left scapular 
spine of two healthy males (P1 and P2). They performed arm elevations, internal rotations, ball throwing, hockey 
shooting, and eating movements. Ellipsoid radii and center location were functionally calibrated for each 
participant and each movement, either based on all frames of a movement or based on a reduced number of 
frames (from 3 to 200 equally position-distributed frames). Across both participants and all movements, ellipsoid 
radii varied up to 10.2 cm, 3.9 cm, and 18.4 cm in the antero-posterior, medio-lateral, and cranio-caudal di-
rections, respectively. When all frames of a movement were considered for calibration, the median scapula-to- 
ellipsoid distance was, on average, 0.52 mm and 0.38 mm for P1 and P2, respectively. When only five frames 
were considered for ellipsoid calibration, the scapula-to-ellipsoid median distance slightly increased with 0.57 
mm and 0.47 mm for P1 and P2, respectively. To conclude, this study highlights that an ellipsoid surface may 
effectively be appropriate to model the scapulothoracic sliding plane, especially when the calibration is func-
tional, participant- and movement-specific. Furthermore, the number of poses required for the ellipsoid cali-
bration can be reduced to five, minimizing the experimental cost. 
1. Introduction 
Scapular kinematics are regularly assessed in the fields of sports 
science (Rogowski et al., 2015), orthopedics (Sella et al., 2022), reha-
bilitation (Hybois et al., 2019) or ergonomics (Goubault et al., 2022). 
Accurate tools based on medical imaging are continuously growing 
(Lawrence et al., 2018; Nicholson et al., 2017; Yoshida et al., 2022); 
nevertheless, their application exposes individuals to radiation, mea-
surement volume remains limited, and is costly, restricting their appli-
cation to a clinical environment. Marker-based stereo-photogrammetry 
is the most common alternative with the use of individual markers 
(Lefebvre et al., 2023) or clusters of markers located either on the 
acromion (Warner et al., 2015) or the scapular spine (Hybois et al., 
2019). These procedures may however be inaccurate because of soft 
tissue artifacts (Blache et al., 2017; Yoshida et al., 2022). In this context, 
marker-based procedures still demand improvement to accurately assess 
scapular kinematics. 
Several numerical procedures have been developed to enhance 
scapular kinematics from markers. Among them, multibody kinematic 
optimization, consisting of optimizing joint angles by minimizing the 
distance between the coordinates of the experimental markers and the 
virtual markers of the kinematic model, has demonstrated promising 
outcomes (Begon et al., 2018). Nevertheless, using open-chain models 
can lead to scapula penetration into the thorax or result in non- 
physiological scapular winging (Maurel and Thalmann, 1999). To 
overcome these issues, most current shoulder kinematic models include 
a closed loop constraining the scapula to maintain contact with the 
thorax, representing the physiological scapulothoracic sliding plane. 
Closed-loop constraints can be implemented using a single or multiple 
contact points-to-ellipsoid surface (Duprey et al., 2017). Nonetheless, 
the calibration procedure for the ellipsoid remains heterogeneous. 
The calibration of the ellipsoid consists of determining the radii and 
the location of the ellipsoid center based on the individual’s anthro-
pometry being evaluated. Several studies (Blana et al., 2008; Bolsterlee

et al., 2014; Charlton and Johnson, 2006; Garner and Pandy, 1999; 
Maurel and Thalmann, 1999; van der Helm, 1994) have employed a 
geometrical approach by adjusting the ellipsoid parameters to fit the 
shape of the thorax surface. This approach is convenient as it requires a 
few key thorax dimensions, which can be easily obtained from skin- 
marker coordinates recorded during a static pose. However, Sah and 
Wang (2009) observed a scapula-to-ellipsoid distance of around 3 cm 
when the ellipsoid is fitted to the thorax shape. In consequence, com-
bined geometrical and functional approaches (Ingram et al., 2016; Pri-
nold et al., 2011) or strictly functional methods (Blache et al., 2022; 
Michaud et al., 2017; Naaim et al., 2017; Seth et al., 2016) have emerged 
for calibrating the ellipsoid parameters. In latter case, the ellipsoid 
surface is fitted to the functional scapulothoracic sliding plane, which is 
usually obtained from additional large-amplitude movements or poses. 
Although this calibration may be more time-consuming, it is believed to 
preserve the bio-fidelity of the scapulothoracic joint. However, to our 
knowledge, no study has quantified the deviation of the scapular tra-
jectory from a functionally-determined ellipsoid surface, ensuring the 
bio-fidelity of a functional calibration. Furthermore, the recommended 
number of poses for ellipsoid calibration is still in debate. 
The aim of this study was twofold: (1) to assess whether an ellipsoid 
surface can model the scapulothoracic sliding plane and (2) to determine 
the optimal number of static poses to calibrate the ellipsoid parameters. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Participants 
Raw data presented by Dal Maso et al. (2014) were used in this 
present study. Two participants (age: 44 and 27 years, Height: 177 and 
165 cm, mass: 82 and 57 kg, for P1 and P2, respectively) volunteered to 
participate and signed informed consent. The data collection was 
approved by the Karolinska Institute (Sweden) and the University of 
Montreal (Canada) ethics committees. None of the participants declared 
any history of shoulder injury. 
2.2. Instrumentation 
An intracortical pin with a rigid cluster of four reflective markers was 
inserted into the left scapular spine under local anesthesia that lasted 
two hours (see Dal Maso et al. (2014) for more details). Four skin 
markers were put on the thorax (7th cervical vertebra, 8th thoracic 
vertebra, xiphoid process, and incisura jugularis), three on the left 
scapula (acromion angle, trigonum spinae, and inferior angle), and two 
on the wrist (ulnar and radial styloid) while the participant was standing 
upright. Three-dimensional marker trajectories were measured using an 
18-camera optoelectronic VICON™ system sampled at 300 Hz (T-40S 
and T-20S; Oxford Metrics Ltd., Oxford, UK). 
2.3. Procedure 
First, the participants held a static anatomical rest position for a few 
seconds. Then they were asked to perform functional movements, 
including arm elevations in the sagittal (n = 2 and 11 repetitions for P1 
and P2, respectively) and frontal planes (n = 2 and 11), humerothoracic 
internal rotations with the arm abducted and the elbow flexed at 90◦ (n 
= 6 and 4); two sports movements, namely, mimicking a ball throwing 
(n = 4 and 4) and hockey shooting (n = 5 and 4); and one daily life 
activity (i.e., eating; n = 3 and 2). The number of repetitions varied 
according to the discomfort each participant felt due to intracortical pins 
felt. All movements were performed at a preferred speed.

three skin marker coordinates recorded during the static pose (acro-
mion, trigonum spinae, and inferior angle). This barycenter was used to 
define the scapula origin’s location relative to the reference system of 
the marker cluster secured to the scapular pin. Then, for each repetition 
of each movement and each participant, a participant- and movement- 
specific ellipsoid was built as follows: the 3D coordinates of the ellip-
soid center in the thorax coordinate system and its three radii ( E→) were 
optimized using a trust-region nonlinear least-squares algorithm, such 
that the 3D scapula-to-ellipsoid distance was minimized (Eq. (1).

To avoid local minima for the optimization solution, a multi-start 
approach with 10 uniformly distributed pseudorandom initial guesses 
was implemented. To assess the influence of the number of frames used 
for ellipsoid calibration, the procedure was repeated with only 3 to 10, 
15, 20, 50, 100, and 200 frames equally distributed from the move-
ment’s first to last frames. To that end, wrist excursion was considered to 
split each movement into equal displacement parts. Finally, the pro-
cedure was also done by considering all the movements together, 
keeping only the middle frame, the last frame, the first and the last 
frame, or all the frames of each movement. 
For each movement and each ellipsoid calibration, the scapula-to- 
ellipsoid distance was computed at each movement frame. The me-
dian and 95th percentiles (P95) values were reported. A scapula-to- 
ellipsoid distance equal to zero meant that the scapula center trajec-
tory followed the surface of an ellipsoid precisely. As the distance in-
creases, the suitability of the ellipsoid surface as a model for the scapula 
center trajectory decreases. 
3. Results 
Depending on the movement and the participant, ellipsoid radii 
varied up to 4.1 cm, 6.0 cm, 16.3 cm in the antero-posterior, medio- 
lateral, and cranio-caudal directions, respectively. The centers of the 
ellipsoids were mostly located anteriorly and on the same side as the 
investigated limb (Table 1). 
When all frames of a movement were considered (up to 551 frames 
and 889 frames for P1 and P2, respectively), scapula-to-ellipsoid dis-
tance was always inferior to 4.1 mm. More precisely, the median 
scapula-to-ellipsoid distance was, on average, 0.6 mm (P95: 1.5 mm),

0.8 mm to 1.3 mm (P95 ranging from 3.3 mm to 5.5 mm). When 
considering all movements together, median scapula-to-ellipsoid dis-
tances were greater than those observed when each movement was 
taken individually, with maximal distances up to 10.4 mm and 11.4 mm 
for P1 and P2, respectively (Table 2). 
4. Discussion 
The main findings of this study were that the trajectory of the scapula 
center is very close to a participant- and movement-specific ellipsoid 
surface. Furthermore, it was determined that a reduced set of static 
poses could be suitable to calibrate ellipsoid parameters. 
Ellipsoid parameters are usually calibrated specifically to the indi-
vidual using either a geometrical (Bolsterlee et al., 2014) or a functional 
(Michaud et al., 2017) approach. In comparison to a previous study, 
which observed a scapula-to-ellipsoid distance of around 3 cm based on 
the geometrical approach (Sah and Wang, 2009), the deviation of the 
scapula center from the ellipsoid surface was approximately 50 times 
smaller when a participant- and movement-specific functional ellipsoid 
is built. Moreover, the observed variability in the values of the ellipsoid 
parameter substantiated the participant-specific nature of the ellipsoid, 
as previously highlighted by Michaud et al. (2017). These findings were 
even true for similar movements (i.e., arm elevation in the sagittal or 
frontal plane), reinforcing that the ellipsoid should be calibrated spe-
cifically to the movement. Finally, when considering all movements 
together for ellipsoid calibration, the median deviations to the ellipsoid 
were the highest with maximal values near 10 mm indicating again the 
necessity for a movement-specific calibration of ellipsoid parameters. As 
a result, the scapulothoracic sliding plane may be modeled by an ellip-
soid surface as long as the latter is customized to both the individual and 
the movement involved. 
The functional calibration of the ellipsoid requires additional poses 
or movements performed by the participant. Nevertheless, the number 
of poses varies across research groups, ranging from 5 to 55, or some-
times remaining unknown (Blache et al., 2022; Michaud et al., 2017; 
Naaim et al., 2017; Seth et al., 2016). When poses were evenly distrib-
uted from the beginning to the end of the movement, reducing their 
number had minimal influence on the scapula-to-ellipsoid distance, as 
deviations less than 2.2 mm were observed between three poses and the 
maximal number of poses used for ellipsoid calibration. In the case of 
ellipsoid calibration with a skin marker-based approach, increasing the 
number of static poses for ellipsoid calibration increases the time of 
experimentation since the participant has to hold a static position for 
few seconds while the examiner palpates the scapula for anatomical 
landmark identification. Our findings seem to suggest that the cost (i.e., 
experimentation time) -benefit (i.e., scapula-to-ellipsoid distance) bal-
ance favors a reduced the number of poses. However, examiners should 
be aware that the fewer poses are used, the more sensitive the ellipsoid 
calibration is to inaccuracies in palpating scapular anatomical land-
marks, suggesting that experienced examiner might be preferred for 
ellipsoid calibration purpose (Goncalves et al., 2019; Lavaill et al., 
2022). Consequently, a reduced number of evenly distributed poses for 
functional ellipsoid calibration could be used to decrease the experi-
mental cost without drastically affecting the accuracy of ellipsoid 
parameters. 
When modeling the scapulothoracic joint with a single point-to- 
ellipsoid contact, a strict or soft constraint may be applied. The 
former, defined either by an equality constraint (Naaim et al., 2017) or a 
mobilizer (Seth et al., 2016), prohibits any deviations of the scapula 
contact point from the ellipsoid surface. In contrast, the soft constraint, 
usually defined in the cost function (Livet et al., 2023; Michaud et al., 
2017) or through viscoelastic joints (Hu et al., 2020), allows slight de-
viations of the scapula from the ellipsoid surface if it improves the 
marker tracking cost. Considering that the scapula-to-ellipsoid distance 
is typically in the millimeter range during 95 % of the movement and 
remains smaller than the marker residuals obtained from standard 
multibody kinematic optimization during the remaining 5 % of the 
movement, a strict constraint would be appropriate if the functional 
calibration is participant- and movement- specific. However, when 
multiple movements are investigated, and a movement-nonspecific 
ellipsoid is calibrated, a soft constraint would also be suitable to 
manage the 5 % of the poses where the deviation from the ellipsoid 
surface reaches 10 mm. Therefore, the procedure chosen to calibrate the 
ellipsoid should be considered when implementing constraints on the 
scapulothoracic joint.

limitation was the small number of participants, limiting the inference of 
our results to a larger population. Nevertheless, the invasive nature of 
the protocol makes the investigation difficult on a large sample, but it 
fosters the analysis of very accurate data. The second limitation was that 
our findings may not be extended to patients suffering from abnormal 
scapular kinematics. Further investigations should be conducted on such 
pathological populations. The third limitation of our study was that we 
did not explore the influence of ellipsoid calibration on the entire pro-
cess of a multibody kinematic optimization for estimating scapular ki-
nematics. Previous studies have indicated that the clavicle length has the 
most significance influence on scapular kinematics estimation (Blache 
et al., 2022; El Habachi et al., 2013). Nevertheless, ellipsoid parameters 
also demonstrated noteworthy effects (Hybois et al., 2017), potentially 
accounting for 11 % of variations in scapular kinematic estimates 
(Blache et al., 2022). Consequently, further studies need to assess the 
effect of ellipsoid calibration on scapular kinematics when employing a 
multibody kinematics optimization approach. 
To conclude, this study highlights that an ellipsoid surface may 
effectively model the scapulothoracic sliding plane, especially when the 
calibration is functional, participant- and movement-specific. Further-
more, the number of poses required for the ellipsoid calibration can be 
reduced, minimizing the experimental cost.
