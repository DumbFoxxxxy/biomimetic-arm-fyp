Abstract. The shoulder complex in the human body consists of the scapula, clavicle,
humerus, and thorax and bears the load imposed by arm movements while at
the same time realizing a wide range of motions. To mimic and exploit its role,
several musculoskeletal robot arms with shoulder complex mechanisms have been
developed. However, although many research groups have tried to design the structures
using links and joints that faithfully correspond to the bones and joints in the
human shoulder complex, its function has not been successfully reproduced because
biologically plausible designs seriously compromise engineering plausibility. In this
paper, we propose a linkage mechanism that can reproduce complex three-dimensional
scapulo movements and considers the trade-oﬀ between biological and engineering
plausibilities. Subsequently, the design was validated by driving the mechanism using
pneumatic articial muscles placed similarly to muscles in humans. Further, we
present experiments in which the robot was controlled by surface electromyographic
signals from a human. We show that the proposed design, due to its kinematic
similarity with human musculoskeletal systems, eases the conversion between the
surface electromyogram signals and the pneumatic articial muscles (PAM) control
inputs.
Keywords: musculoskeletal robot, pneumatic articial muscles, shoulder complex,
surface electromyography.

methods. Although methods of developing musculoskeletal robot arms with shoulder
complex mechanisms that faithfully mimic the bones and the joints have been studied
[3, 4, 5], it has been very diﬃcult to realize the function of the shoulder complex in
robots. In particular, the complex movements of the shoulder complex, especially about
the three DOFs movements of the scapula, has been diﬃcult to reproduce.

realize the humanlike shoulder complex. On the other hand, a biologically-inspired
engineering design, which is not purely mimicking biological systems, but focuses on
engineering systems to have the same function as the system by taking inspiration
from the biological knowledge, has not been established. For instance, considering
implementations of an elbow joint and/or a wrist joint that have only one DOF, many
musculoskeletal robots employ an easily engineered hinge joint instead of employing the
biomimetic joint that would consist of a fossa and a head of two linkages, ligaments, an
articular capsule, and tendons because it has been recognized that the hinge joint is the
satisable biologically-inspired engineering design of these joints. If the construction
of the joint itself was not the focus of the robot design, it is clear that the use of
hinge joint is the better option in terms of simplicity and reliability. Considering the
implementation of the shoulder complex in terms of the example above, if its complex
three DOFs movement is achieved by a mechanism consisting of linkages, hinges, sliders,
and other well-established mechanical components, it will provide the important and
useful alternative to design a shoulder complex mechanism of a musculoskeletal robot,
while maintaining the original function. In this research, we assume that maintaining
the DOFs corresponds to the biologically-inspired engineering design of the shoulder
complex. Alternative designs presented in literature usually employ a spherical joint
corresponding to only the GH joint, which could be considered as a too much simplied
implementation of the shoulder complex, and as a diﬃcult technical challenge because
of the complex three-dimensional movements of the scapula.
In this paper, we propose a linkage mechanism that can reproduce the complex
three-dimensional scapulo movements and simultaneously considers the trade-oﬀ
between biological and engineering plausibilities. The remainder of this paper is
organized as follows. Section 2 discusses related work conducted on the shoulder complex
in the elds of biomechanics, physiology, and robotics. Section 3 presents the proposed
linkage mechanism that can realize the complex motions of the shoulder complex. To
validate the design, the mechanism is driven by pneumatic articial muscles that are
placed similarly to muscles in humans. Section 4 outlines an experiment in which a robot
is controlled by the surface electromyographic signals of a human, and which shows that
a simple linear regression or a neural network can accomplish the signal conversion from
surface electromyogram (sEMG) signals to the control inputs thanks to the kinematic
similarity with the human musculoskeletal systems. Section 5 concludes this paper.

2.1. Shoulder complex in humans
As shown in Figure 1, the shoulder complex mainly consists of four parts and four joints.
In the shoulder complex, the scapula and the humerus have been focused on as bones
that exhibit distinctive movements [1]. In addition, several observations and hypotheses
about the relative movement of the humerus to the scapula exist [6, 7].
In this complex structure, the ST joint, which is a contact between the scapula and
the thorax, is the part whose function is the most diﬃcult to understand and reproduce.
It consists of a functional joint that does not have an articular capsule and cartilages
but functionally behaves as a joint. Thus, it cannot exhibit the function without the
contribution of muscles, in contrast with anatomical joints, which are more commonly
known as joints. Because there is a muscle between the scapula and the thorax, it is
assumed that the ST joint constrains the scapula surface to the surface of the thorax
[8]. In robotics terms, the ST joint is viewed as a two DOFs constraint of the scapula.
Because there are additional DOF constraints provided by the AC and the SC joints,
the original six DOFs of the scapula accordingly become a three-DOFs joint. Figure
2 shows the corresponding three-DOFs motions of the scapula. Because conventional
spherical joints are suﬃcient to imitate the SC and the AC joints, the scapula motion
can be reproduced if the mechanical design of the ST joint is opportunely devised.
Many biomechanical and physiological works can be found in the literature. In
contrast, quantitative data on the motions of the scapula are diﬃcult to nd. In [1],
qualitative data about the protraction/retraction motion, such as that shown in Table

and stability are better than those of the spherical joints. Although it is essentially
the connection between the scapula and the humerus, several contact points between
the scapula and the humerus are involved with motion, due to the complex shape of
the scapula. In other words, the contact point can be switched to other appropriate
positions when the humerus performs wide movements, so that both a wide ROM and
high stability are maintained. Therefore, a joint that has the equivalent ROM is an
additional requirement of the proposed shoulder complex linkage mechanism.

2.2. Shoulder complex mechanism in musculoskeletal robots
Several musculoskeletal robots with shoulder complex mechanisms have been developed
to date [4, 5, 9, 10, 11, 12]. However, the main focus in those cases was on how the scapula
makes contact with the thorax. In the ST joint of humans, these two parts can move very
smoothly and stably while the contact surface helps reducing the contact pressure as a
result of the muscles between these two parts. Because it is still very diﬃcult to mimic
the constitution of the ST joint in engineering terms, these musculoskeletal robots have
often employed simplied designs of the ST joints. For instance, the surface contact were
realized using spherically simplied shapes of the scapula and the thorax by Sodeyama
et al. [9]. Ikemoto et al. [11] focused on the trade-oﬀ between the contact status
and the shape imitation to achieve suﬃcient ROM and stability. However, despite the
diﬀerence in their designs, these works present the same underlying concept of having
the ST joint as a contact between two parts corresponding to the scapula and the
thorax. This requires that actuators be attached to the shoulder complex mechanism
in order to exert suﬃcient force to maintain the scapula on the thorax. Otherwise, the
scapula cannot move smoothly or can lose the function of the ST joint by leaving from
the thorax. Although this is the same requirement that the human shoulder complex
satises, it is very diﬃcult to imitate the complex control of actuators. In general, to
realize joints by pulling a part on another part is a faithful imitation of joints in animals,
but the engineering imitation cannot achieve a similar performance. Therefore, most of
the abovementioned robots use spherical joints, hinge joints, and/or their combinations
because no other meaningful alternative engineering design has been presented.
The shoulder complex linkage mechanism proposed in this paper is the rst to
imitate the complex scapula movement as a linkage mechanism with a completely
diﬀerent design from the actual mechanism, but which provides a similar function in an
engineering-valid design.
In addition to the ST joint, the GH joint, which connects the scapula and the
humerus, excels in providing a very wide ROM compared to spherical engineering joints.
There are two types of implementations for the GH joint. Let us call the rst one, which
consists of a ball and a cup corresponding to the head and the fossa of bones, respectively,
the open joint. In the works related to musculoskeletal robots cited above, few robots
have the open joint as the GH joint [13, 5]. The open joint can have a wide ROM
and high anatomical plausibility. On the other hand, abarticulation can result from the
absence of supports from other soft tissue such as ligaments, muscles, and an articular
capsule. Because the ST joint is diﬃcult to implement by simply mimicking its structure,
the open joint is still diﬃcult to use for the GH joint in terms of current engineering
technologies. Instead of the open joint, many musculoskeletal robots have employed
commercially available joints as their GH joints. Because they are not aﬀected by the
abarticulation problem, we call the second type of joint the close joint. A typical close
joint is a spherical joint that provides three DOFs with the same rotation center used
in [14]. However, compared to the GH joint, it does not have enough ROM because

the cup that covers the ball is suﬃcient to prevent abarticulation. Alternatively, in
[4, 15], the three DOFs of the GH joint are realized by a combination of joints that have
less than three DOFs. This can provide enough ROM without abarticulation, but the
torque around each axis can diﬀer when actuators are attached because of the kinematic
diﬀerence. To address this problem, we previously proposed a ʠpartly-open jointʡ[11].
The proposed joint also consists of a cup and a ball, but claws attached around the cup
hold the ball. If the link attached to the ball part slants and collides with the claws,
the claws can avoid stopping the link motion by passively rotating with the cup part.
However, because its current realization is still too large to be mounted on the scapula,
downsizing is required.
In the current study, we adopted the partly-open joint by downsizing the structure
and mounting it on the scapula linkage of the shoulder complex linkage mechanism
developed. Because the mechanism does not suﬀer from abarticulation and does not
need actuator control to maintain the joint functions, it allows us to implement and
control actuators by simply considering the motions.
2.3. Robot control based on sEMG signals.
sEMG has often been viewed as a meaning of controlling a robot ʟs motions intuitively
by non-professional users. In brief, the main problem in realizing sEMG-based robot
control is construction of a mapping from the sEMG signals of the user to the robot ʟ
s input signals. Because of diﬀerences between humans and robots, such as kinematic
diﬀerences, the mapping is usually strongly nonlinear. Although a control system based
on the kinematic and/or the dynamics model is the most straightforward approach
[16, 17], it is not always realistically applicable if complex robots that have many
DOFs are the control target. To overcome this problem, nonlinear mapping techniques
have been employed, such as feedforward neural networks [18, 19] and support vector
machines [20, 21, 22]. Because the nonlinearity is believed to be derived from the
kinematic diﬀerence between the mechanical structures of humans and robots, if they
are similar to each other, the mapping becomes easier.
In our study, to validate the shoulder complex linkage mechanism developed, we
installed pneumatic articial muscles (PAMs) onto the mechanism and conducted an
sEMG-based control experiment. The mechanical similarity and the linkage mechanism
(which does not require actuators to keep the joint function) enabled the mapping will
be simplied.

4. Experimental evaluation
In this section, we describe an experiment conducted to validate the design of the
shoulder complex linkage mechanism. To control the postures of the shoulder complex
linkage mechanism in order to be able to assume the same posture as a human, a
method that appropriately transforms sEMG signals to desired pressures is required. In
this section, we outline an experiment conducted in which a linear regression model and
a conventional feedforward neural network were employed to construct the mapping.
If the shoulder complex linkage mechanism has high similarity to that of humans, the
mapping from sEMG signals to desired pressures is assumed to be simple because of
the kinematic similarity. Therefore, if the linear regression model can exhibit equivalent

===== PAGE 14 =====
Shoulder complex linkage mechanism for humanlike musculoskeletal robot arms 14
performance to the neural network, this would mean that the mapping is simple and
that the shoulder complex linkage mechanism developed was successfully designed to
have high similarity with that of humans.
In this section, we rst explain how the supervised data were obtained. We then
present our results and the analysis.
4.1. Experimental setup
To construct a mapping from sEMG signals to desired pressures, it is necessary to acquire
corresponding datasets, obtained while both the shoulder complex linkage mechanism
and the human shoulder complex are taking the same postures. However, because their
kinematics models are very complex, it is very diﬃcult to obtain the corresponding
datasets analytically. To avoid this diﬃculty, we employed the following procedure to
obtain the datasets:
(i) Actuate the PAMs to take 12 diﬀerent postures by manually changing the desired
pressures and recording them.
(ii) Get a subject to mimic these 12 diﬀerent postures in three diﬀerent loading
conditions and record the sEMG for two seconds.
(iii) Combine these sEMG signals and the desired pressures, which correspond to the
same posture for the human and the robot, respectively.
Table 4 shows the 12 diﬀerent postures of the shoulder complex linkage mechanism used
in step 1. In step 2, the subject held 0, 1, and 2 [Kg] weights while keeping the elbow
fully extended. Table 5 shows the target muscles that were measurable using sEMG
sensors and which contributed to the shoulder complex movements. Note that PAMs
attached on the shoulder complex linkage mechanism are arranged to roughly correspond
to these muscles. Between each trial, the subject rested for at least 60 seconds. In step
3, 36 data points corresponding to 12 diﬀerent postures were obtained as the supervised
dataset.

Despite the fact that the number of supervised data points was insuﬃcient, the

mechanism developed has similar kinematic properties to that of the human shoulder
complex.
5. Conclusion
In this paper, we proposed a linkage mechanism that can reproduce complex three-
dimensional scapulo movements and which considers the trade-oﬀ between biological
and engineering plausibilities. The shoulder complex linkage mechanism developed
exhibits a wide range of motion equivalent to that of the human scapula. For further
validation, we installed PAMs controlled by PID controllers and arranged similar to
muscles in humans. In the experiment, surface electromyogram (sEMG) based control,
which computes the desired pressures from sEMG signals obtained from corresponding
muscles in a subject, was tested by using both a linear regression model and a neural
network. The results of analysis showed that there was no clear diﬀerence between these
linear and nonlinear methods. This suggests that the mapping from sEMG signals to
desired pressures was simplied as a result of the shoulder complex linkage mechanism.
The validity of the shoulder complex linkage mechanism developed was thus successfully
conrmed in terms of both range of motion and kinematics. Although it is necessary to
increase the number of subjects and to apply statistical investigations to derive stronger
conclusions, we believe the results presented in this paper are important contributions
to the design of musculoskeletal robots.
