APPLIED SCIENCES AND ENGINEERING
Encoded sewing soft textile robots
Xinyu Guo1†, Wenbo Li2*†, Fuyi Fang1, Huyue Chen3, Linchuan Zhao1, Xiaoyong Fang1, Zhiran Yi1, 
Lei Shao3, Guang Meng1, Wenming Zhang1,4*
Incorporating soft actuation with soft yet durable textiles could effectively endow the latter with active and flex -
ible shape morphing and motion like mollusks and plants. However, creating highly programmable and customiz-
able soft robots based on textiles faces a longstanding design and manufacturing challenge. Here, we report a 
methodology of encoded sewing constraints for efficiently constructing three- dimensional (3D) soft textile robots 
through a simple 2D sewing process. By encoding heterogeneous stretching properties into three spatial seams 
of the sewed 3D textile shells, nonlinear inflation of the inner bladder can be guided to follow the predefined 
spatial shape and actuation sequence, for example, tendril- like shape morphing, tentacle- like sequential manipu-
lation, and bioinspired locomotion only controlled by single pressure source. Such flexible, efficient, scalable, and 
low- cost design and formation methodology will accelerate the development and iteration of soft robots and also 
open up more opportunities for safe human- robot interactions, tailored wearable devices, and health care.
INTRODUCTION
Textiles incorporating functions such as active deformation (1), dis-
play (2), thermal management (3), sensing (4), and power (5) have 
attracted extensive attention recently. The derivation of smart tex-
tiles with multifunctionality, high integration, and intelligence is 
revolutionizing soft materials and wearable technology. For exam-
ple, through incorporating soft actuation technologies (shape mem-
ory alloy, artificial muscle, electroactive polymer, etc.) with soft yet 
durable textiles, actuating or active textiles capable of reacting and 
adapting to external environments and stimuli through active mor-
phing or motion can be developed (6–12). The newly endowed ac-
tuation characteristic for conventional passive textiles is mainly 
dependent on the performance of soft actuators and the structural 
design of textiles and their coupling effects. Compared to other soft 
actuators, great progress and wide application have been achieved 
by soft pneumatic actuators (SPAs) due to their advantages of large 
pressure- induced deformation and force, high energy density, fast 
response, low cost, large design freedom, and versatile modes of ac-
tuation (13–16). Different from the elastomer- based SPAs which are 
usually fabricated by the complex and time- consuming molding- 
casting process (17–19) or the costly three- dimensional (3D) print-
ing (20–22), the textile- based SPAs can be easily manufactured by 
wrapping a constraint textile shell around a bladder or bonding off- 
the- shelf composite fabrics with different anisotropic mechanics 
properties. Moreover, textile- based SPAs naturally inherit the ad-
vantages of textiles such as light weight, flexibility, compliance, du-
rability, and supporting customization which are ideal for human- robot 
interactions and tailored wearable applications (23–26). However, the 
efficient design and low- cost manufacturing of textile- based SPAs 
and soft textile robots (STRs) with highly programmable and con-
trollable 3D shape morphing and complex motions are longstand-
ing and recognized challenges (23, 24). As we know, the diversity of 
achievable motions is crucial for multifunctional devices, especially 
for wearable devices that require ease of customization for different 
body parts and individuals (27, 28). Poor fit or casual placement of 
components or actuators can reduce the performance and limit the 
function of wearable devices (23).
One of the main challenges in textile- based soft robotics is the 
creation of anisotropic mechanics (24). From the design and manu-
facturing point of view, that is how to create textile- based constraint 
shells for STRs with specific programmed deformations and mo-
tions. However, the existing method based on splicing different 2D 
anisotropy fabrics (woven, knitted, and nonwoven) with different 
mechanical properties by conventional cut- and- sew or heat- assisted 
sealing processes (29, 30) has design limitations in 3D morphing and 
multidimensional motion and also increases the modeling and for-
mation complexities. Although some promising developments, such 
as structuring 3D textile shells with locally adjustable mechanical 
properties through 3D knitting (31– 33) to preprogram the global 
constraints for inflation, or constructing active textile sheets bottom- 
up by weaving, knitting, or sewing (34– 36), complex preparation 
processes or specialized equipment are required which undoubtedly 
increase the cost and may cause other manufacturing challenges 
(24). In addition, large inflated profiles and ballooning of the inner 
bladder when yarn density is low also limit the desired deformation 
and output force. The lack of efficient and systematic design and 
modeling methodology leads to time- consuming prototyping and 
iteration cycles for customization (23).
To solve the above problem and challenge, we introduce a dis-
tinctive design methodology of encoded sewing constraint (ESC) 
for efficiently structuring the 3D textile shells with programmed 
global strain constraint for STRs. As shown in Fig. 1, we have devel-
oped a series of multifunctional STRs based on the ESC method and 
pneumatic actuation which fully demonstrate the remarkable features 
of high- dimensional programmability (both the shape morphing 
and the actuation sequence), customizable design, and easy manu-
facturing of STRs. Different from most of the previous work that 
only used sewing as a bonding method, we notice that the stretch-
ability of knit fabrics can be regulated by sewing with different stitch 
features which offers us the possibility to achieve the bonding and 
strain constraining simultaneously during the formation of seams. 
Two typical types of stitches are coded as binary “0” and “1” which 
respectively indicate two kinds of distinct tensile properties of the

Principle and verification of encoded sewing constraint
Weft- knit fabric exhibits more elasticity and has great potential for pro-
grammability in the weft direction. The fabrics sewed with different 
stitches along the weft direction show distinct tensile properties affected 
by stitch type and density (Fig. 3, A and B). The straight stitch with a sew-
ing density of 8 SPI (stitch per inch) is coded as “0” representing the 
strong strain constraint for the fabric. Relatively, more stretchable stitch-
es such as zigzag with different densities are coded as “1” representing 
the tunable stretchability of the sewed fabrics. We systematically inves-
tigated the effect of ESC with different stitch features on the mechanical 
properties of the knit fabric (see figs. S1 and S2). Figure 3B displays the 
stress- strain curves of the original knit fabric without stitching and 
fabrics with different sewing constraints, which can provide fundamental 
guidance for the constraint design.

When the constraint fabric is extended to the strength limitation by 
the internal pressure (170 kPa), its bending curvature reaches a sta-
ble maximum value which is negatively correlated with the density 
of the zigzag stitch. On the basis of this, we demonstrate continuous 
meandering deformation of a tailored 2D STR whose textile shell is 
simply fabricated by sewing two same pieces of knit fabric along the 
encoded seam (Fig.  3E and movie S2), which is very tedious and 
difficult using the conventional design and formation method (29, 
37). The corresponding simulation also shows a good agreement 
with the practical deformation. More simulation details can be 
found in Materials and Methods and fig. S3.
We then analyze the effect of the distance d between the seams 
on the deformation properties of STRs by basic 2D bending 
units. The deformation characteristics of the basic 2D bending units 
with different design parameters d driven by pneumatic pressure 
are shown in fig. S4E. The stable maximum bending curvature 
values of the bending units are negatively correlated with the dis-
tance d, suggesting that d also programs the bending behavior 
of STRs.
We also found that the design parameter d can program the 
bending sequence for the STRs due to the coupling effect between 
the constraint shell and the nonlinear inflation of the internal 
bladder. The bladders are “unstable” during inflation (38). Once 
inflated, the internal air pressure of the bladder first increases until 
the bladder deforms notably; however, the internal air pressure then 
decreases as the subsequent deformation. Thus, for bladders that are 
coupled to each other, the bladder with the largest initial deforma-
tion can be the first to deform substantially, while the others have to 
“wait. ” The internal bladder of STR comes into contact with the 2D 
shell during expansion, its initial expansion tendency and final ex-
pansion radius are determined by the dimensions of the 2D shell 
(fig. S5A). Therefore, the expanding tendency of the inflating blad-
der in the “unstable” state is ultimately determined by distance d 
(fig. S5B). Once inflated, the portion of the bladder corresponding 
to the largest d will be the first to inflate and drive the deformation 
of the corresponding region of the STR. As the bladder expands to 
the dimensional limit of the 2D shell, its internal air pressure rises 
rapidly, and the remaining portion of the bladder can continue to 
deform sequentially (fig. S5C). The radius- pressure relationship of 
the bladder can be derived from theoretical analysis (see the “Non-
linear inflation of the bladder” section in Materials and Methods). 
The inflatable deformation behavior of STRs affected by d can thus 
be predicted by theory. The experimental data fall at the correspond-
ing theoretical curves and their starting points indicate the critical 
pressures for bending (Fig. 3F). On the basis of the above quantita-
tive knowledge, we can easily program the deformation curvature 
and sequence of 2D STRs to demonstrate the shape- shifting be-
tween different patterns of Arabic numerals such as “1”- “7”- “8” 
and “1”- “0”- “6” (Fig.  3G and movie S3) and the sequential 
morphing of an STR for navigating through obstacles and en-
vironmental monitoring (Fig. 3H and movie S4), all controlled by 
single pressure source.
Design and characterizing of 3D STRs
The ESC also provides an effective way for structuring 3D textile 
shells for STRs simply by sewing three same pieces of fabrics 
(fig. S4A); three encoded seams can guide the spatial deformation 
and inflation sequence of the internal bladder. Figure 4A illustrates 
the simulation verification for the spatial deformation principle of 
the 3D morphing unit. Theoretically, the unit can be designed to 
bend in an arbitrary direction by adjusting the stitch features of the 
three seams as depicted in cross- sectional schematics. The bending 
direction can be predicted by the energy minimization principle 
(see the “Modeling of the STR” section in Materials and Methods). 
To simplify the analysis, the stitching on one of the seam edges of 
the 3D morphing unit is varied, as shown in Fig. 4B, and this vari-
able seam edge is labeled with “x . ” The experimental data and the 
simulation results fall on the theoretical curve, indicating the highly 
programmable and predictable design of the 3D ESC. Figure 4C 
shows larger stitch density corresponding to more stretchable seams 
leading to bigger bending curvature under the same pressure 
(e.g., 100 kPa) for a 3D morphing unit with one “0” seam and 
two identical “1” seams. The results for other cases are shown in 
fig. S4 (F and H).
Figure 4D shows the effect of design parameter d (distance 
between adjacent seams) on the deformation sequence and curva-
ture of the 3D morphing unit, showing good agreement with our 
theoretical models (see “Modeling of the STR” section in Materials 
and Methods). More details can be found in fig. S4 (G and I). We 
also tested the block force of different units with the same length 
(100 mm), as shown in Fig. 4E, a unit with d = 17 mm can generate 
force beyond 5 N, and the force or the bending stiffness increases 
with pressure and d.
Similarly, on the basis of the above quantitative knowledge, we 
can design and manufacture STRs with more complex 3D shape 
morphing and sequence actuation abilities, as shown in Fig. 2. 
We also demonstrate two kinds of 3D spiral STRs, one with multiple 
curvatures and the other with constant curvature (Fig. 4F and 
movie S5). The spiral STR can be used as a soft gripper for winding 
objects with varying shapes and sizes. Figure 4G also shows the 
contraction actuation performance of the spiral STR, l/l0 (see Fig. 4F) 
increases with the pressure and stitch density, and a maximum value 
of about 70% can be achieved corresponding to 90- SPI stitch den-
sity and 130- kPa pressure.
Customized multifunctional STRs

to demonstrate the flexibility, effectiveness, and scalability of the ESC 
method. For example, tailored 2D STRs can respectively morph into 
different patterns to match the profiles of a vase (Fig. 5A) and a but-

sequential flexion of the five fingers following inflation (Fig. 5C and 
fig. S6D), a butterfly- like robot that can mimic the shape and the 
flapping motion (Fig. 5D), both are controlled by the single pressure 
source. Figure 5 (E and F) shows two kinds of customizable wearable 
applications based on STRs, one is a thumb rehabilitation glove

DISCUSSION
We have demonstrated an efficient and systematic design and manu-
facturing methodology for highly programmable and customizable 
3D STRs based on the proposed ESC. The unique design of encoded 
seams with preprogrammed tensile property not only enables ubiq-
uitous textiles to structure 3D constraint shells with simple, 2D manu-
facturing methods but also substantially simplifies the prediction 
difficulty for complex 3D morphing. By simply regulating the stitch 
features for seams, we can achieve high- dimensional programma-
bility for STRs, both the shape morphing and the inflation sequence. 
Leveraging the highly programmable feature, we not only demon-
strate diverse shape morphing and multifunctional STRs but also 
develop several bioinspired soft robots for complex manipulation 
(such as winding and grasping) and locomotion. We realize the con-
tinuous rolling, bidirectional crawling, and tube crawling locomo-
tion respectively by using only one actuator and one control signal, 
which verify the high physical intelligence of our proposed ESC 
method and the STRs for substantially reducing the hardware com-
plexity of soft robots. In addition, due to the natural compatibility of 
STRs with textiles, the creation of connected arrays for the STRs can 
be easily achieved by sewing, such as a pneumatic textile flower 
(fig. S6, E and F , and movie S14). Our proposed ESC method is a ready- 
to- use method, with no prior preparation for fabricating and modi-
fying special fibers and adjustments for equipment. It also provides 
efficient design guidance and a simplified fabrication process for 
diverse customized requirements.
Of course, the viscoelastic of the embedded elastic fibers (like 
spandex) and plastic deformation of the fabric used in STRs are in-
evitable during the usage, just like the edge curl and shape change of 
the clothes when worn for a long time. The hysteresis behavior caused 
by the effects of friction and entanglement between the constraint 
textile shell and the internal bladder can also affect the deformation 
and recovery of the STRs. Basically, these are the common issues for 
textile- based SPAs toward practical applications, along with the 
problems of working life, robustness, and performance degenera-
tion in long- term cyclic actuation (23). However, for some cases that 
the requirement of deformation precision is not high, such as the 
rehabilitation and robot locomotion, adjusting the control pressure 
to compensate or eliminate the adverse effect of the viscoelastic and 
hysteresis for the STRs is also a feasible way for achieving the cor -
responding functionalities.
Overall, instead of relying on complex processes and costly 
equipment, the methodology based on ESC solves the challenge of 
highly customizable design and low- cost manufacturing of mul-
tifunctional STRs only by the traditional cut- and- sew processing 
with off- the- shelf textiles. The introduction of advanced computerized 
sewing techniques may open up more opportunities for customiz -
able mass manufacturing. Moreover, the effective quantitative frame-
work for modeling and predicting of 3D STRs also accelerates the 
rapid transition of prototype devices from laboratory to consumer 
products. More generally, this distinctive ESC methodology is scalable 
and versatile and shows substantial progress in the design and fabri-
cation of active textiles and soft robots (see table S1). We envision 
that integrating sensing, logic, communication, and powering capa-
bilities into our STRs will accelerate the development of the next 
generation of customizable smart textile robots for a variety of real- 
world applications.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Textile preparation
We used the highly elastic anisotropic fabric (1 × 1 weft- knit fabric 
consisting of 95% cotton and 5% spandex, produced on a double- sided 
circular weft- knitting machine) which is common and commercial-
ly available as the basic textile. The 1 × 1 knit fabric is knitted with a 
front coil warp row and a back coil warp row in an interleaved con-
figuration, which is made from 40s yarns with a density of 80  × 50 
(80 yarns per inch in the warp direction and 50 yarns per inch in the 
weft direction). The knit fabric has a thickness of about 1 mm and a 
density of 180 g/m2. Its micrographs in the initial and tensile states 
with and without sewing constraints are shown in fig. S1. A piece of 
knit fabric with the size of 120 cm by 15 cm is about $1.1, which can 
be cut and sewed to make six 3D shells for the spiral STRs as shown 
in Fig. 4F . On average, the total cost of a 60- cm- long spiral STR is 
about $0.25 adding a latex balloon and ignoring the cost of sewing 
thread. If the quantity purchased is large, then there will be a lower 
unit price.
To facilitate the sewing process, the textile is cut into strips along 
the radial direction of the weft- knit fabric with reference to pre-
defined dimensions, which need to contain the required area for the 
designed stitch. To avoid unintended deformations caused by twist-
ing during actuation, it is necessary to ensure that the textile orien-
tation is not distorted as much as possible so that the stitches are 
perpendicular or parallel to the textile orientation during the sewing 
process.

Biaxial tensile testing of weft- knit fabrics with

MATLAB (MathWorks). We fix two of the custom- made fixtures 
to the carrier table and then fix force sensors (DayangChuangan,

the fixtures.
For the biaxial stretch test, we used cross- weft- knit fabric sam-

sample arm size is 50 mm long, 15 mm wide, and 1 mm thick. The

camera (SONY , CX900E), and then the strains in the warp and weft 
directions of the weft- knit fabric in the marked area are calculated. 
On the basis of the tensile force measured by the sensor, the textile 
stress is calculated (fig. S3).

STR testing
We use a pressure regulator (CPCAC, IR2020- 02BG) to regulate and 
stabilize the air pressure inside the STR, combined with a barometer 
(Panasonic, DP- 101) to determine in real time that the pressure inside 
the STR is accurately stabilized at the value required for our mea-
surements. To quantify the degree of STR deformation, we recorded 
the state of the STR at each air pressure with a camera (SONY , 
CX900E). We then postprocessed the photos using MATLAB 
(MathWorks) to extract the STR centerline to fit the curvature and 
then calculated the bending angle per unit length of the STR.
To quantify the bending stiffness, a 50- mm section at the end of 
the STR is fixed, and the remaining 100- mm section is used for in-
flatable bending. A wall is placed in a horizontal plane and a force 
sensor is attached to the wall to measure the magnitude of the STR 
output force as the STR bends and compresses the wall.
