Abstract
In the fields of robotics and biomechanics, exploring artificial muscle structures that
simulate the functions of human muscles has always been an important research direc-
tion. However, the existing humanoid robots often fail to get closer to human mobility
and simulate the performance of human muscles. To solve this problem, we propose
a method for designing artificial upper limb models inspired by the contraction mech-
anism of human muscles. This model consists of an upper limb skeletal model and a
lattice muscle biomimetic structure, including models of the left upper limb and chest
of the human body, as well as biomimetic muscle structure models of muscles such as
the biceps brachii, triceps brachii and deltoid. The electronic control system struc-
turally controls the bionic muscles through a stepper motor and a spiral slide table in a
linear drive mode. The innovation of this muscle bionic structure lies in the combina-
tion of the linear drive mechanism, the X-lattice elastic structure and the TPU flexible
material, and simulates the muscle contraction effect by designing the deformation of
the X-lattice. Its internal pattern structure has been specially designed and exhibits
unique performance at different deformation stages. The characteristics in the X, Y,
and Z directions ensure the effectiveness of the structure. Furthermore, in this study,
polynomial fitting of the upper limb model was used to examine the relationship be-
tween the motor mechanisms driving various muscle groups in different postures. The
results show that the musculoskeletal robotic arm can basically reproduce the activities
of the human arm. In conclusion, this research provides a new approach for the design
of artificial upper limb models. Future work will focus on optimizing the modeling and
control of different muscle groups in the human body, seeking materials with better
performance, aiming to enhance the flexibility and adaptability of robots and expand
their applications in areas such as assistance and rehabilitation.
KEY WORDS : Biomimetic, Robotic Arm, Humanoid, Cable-Driven

1.1 Motivation
In recent years, research on biomimetic dexterous hands and upper limb robotic arms
has flourished in the field of biomimetic robots, driven by various demands and sci-
entific exploration. These motivations cover key areas such as medical rehabilitation,
industrial manufacturing, scientific research, and special task execution, aiming to
solve many challenges in the real world and promote innovation and breakthroughs in
related technologies.
Biomimetic arms are of great significance in improving prosthetic mobility, restoring
limb function, and other medical rehabilitation fields. Patients with upper limb dis-
abilities have an urgent need to restore limb function and improve their ability to
live independently. Biomimetic robot technology provides a new way to achieve this
goal. According to the research of Chu Chengzhong et al.[ 1], the SMA artificial muscle
biomimetic dexterous hand they designed has the characteristics of high fidelity, real-
istic movements, and stable work. This bionic hand can simulate the fine movements
of the human hand, such as grasping and pinching, and is expected to help patients
with upper limb disabilities complete basic daily operations such as eating, dressing,
writing, etc., greatly improving their quality of life.
In addition, the intelligent bionic arm developed by Open Bionics has achieved better
interaction with the human nervous system through advanced technology, allowing
patients to control the movement of prostheses more naturally and appearing closer
to real arms, reducing the psychological burden of patients wearing prostheses and
further promoting their integration into social and daily life [ 2].
In the field of industrial manufacturing, bionic robotic arms can also meet the needs
of improving production eﬀiciency and adapting to diverse demands. Modern indus-
trial manufacturing demands extremely high precision, flexibility, and eﬀiciency in the

1.2 Aims and Objective
Inspired by the natural contraction mechanism of human muscles, this article will
design and implement a mesh biomimetic muscle structure with high stretchability and
anisotropy. In this design, the mechanism of human muscle contraction is imitated by
combining a flexible and deformable lattice structure with a line driven mechanism.
This structure has anisotropic properties and a wide range of motion, allowing it to
operate under contact forces and flexible deformation. The rope connected to the
motor will control the contraction and extension of muscles, enabling the joints of the
arm to perform functions such as weight. Due to the rope driven method, the driving
device can be remotely driven. Therefore, robotic arms will be able to grasp larger
or heavier objects. Zhe Xu and Emanuel Todorov [ 5] emphasized that traditional

Chapter 2
Literature Review
With the vigorous development of robotics, the research on biomimetic musculoskeletal
robots has increasingly become the focus of attention in both academia and industry.
To make the movement of robots more similar to that of humans, simulating the
performance of human muscles has become a key research direction. By conducting
bio-inspired design of the structure and function of the musculoskeletal system, the
flexibility, adaptability, and movement eﬀiciency of robots in complex environments
can be significantly enhanced, which has great application potential in fields such as
medical rehabilitation, human-robot collaboration, and disaster rescue.
In recent years, researchers have been committed to imitating the human muscu-
loskeletal structure, promoting the continuous development of musculoskeletal robots.
For example, robots like Kengoro and Musashi adopt a rope-driven system and are
equipped with more than 70 ”muscles”, achieving human-like movement through the
coordinated cooperation between the skeletal structure and muscles [ 8]. The ECCE1
robot has a rope-driven upper body structure [ 9], and the Roboy innovatively combines
rope-driven and biological tendons, providing new ideas for the movement control of
robots [ 10]. The lower limb robot developed by Kurumaya et al. [ 11] uses multi-
filament McKibben muscles to achieve simple leg movements , the shoulder complex
designed by Ikemoto et al. uses pneumatic actuators [ 12], and the Athlete Robot
also uses pneumatic actuators to complete dynamic actions [ 13]. It can be seen that
most of the driving methods of bio-inspired musculoskeletal robots focus on two types:
pneumatic driving and cable driving.
In the realm of musculoskeletal robotics, pneumatic actuation has emerged as a preva-
lent and influential mode of actuation. Prominent examples include the shoulder
complex devised by Ikemoto et al. [ 12] and the Athlete Robot [ 13], both of which in-
corporate pneumatic actuators to facilitate their functional movements. Additionally,
Guo and his research cohort have engineered highly programmable and customizable
5

===== PAGE 14 =====
Chapter 2. Literature Review School of Advanced Technology
pneumatic fabric soft robots utilizing textile-based materials. These innovative robotic
systems not only exemplify the inherent flexibility, operational eﬀiciency, scalability,
and cost-effective design attributes of pneumatic actuation but also present novel op-
portunities for secure human-robot interaction, personalized wearable devices, and
healthcare applications [ 14]. Pneumatic actuation confers several significant advan-
tages to robotic systems. Its rapid response time, high power-to-mass ratio, and rel-
atively low cost render it an eﬀicient power generation solution. However, pneumatic
artificial muscles are accompanied by notable limitations. The compressibility of gases
and the structural design constraints of the actuators inherently restrict the range of
motion, thereby limiting the dexterity and flexibility of robotic movements. Moreover,
the compressible nature of gases poses substantial challenges in achieving precise con-
trol over muscle morphology, impeding the creation of intricate and human-like muscle
shapes that closely mimic the complexity and functionality of biological counterparts
[15].
Cable-driven systems have been extensively employed in musculoskeletal robotics, pri-
marily due to their unique advantages in replicating human-like muscle movements.
For instance, the multi-filament McKibben muscles utilized in the lower-limb robot de-
veloped by Kurumaya et al. [ 11] essentially represent a form of cable-driven actuation.
This mechanism mimics muscle contraction and relaxation through the retraction and
release of cables, thereby enabling leg movements. Zhe Xu and Emanuel Todorov [ 5]
also leveraged cable-driven technology to fabricate a dexterous robotic hand, where
bio-inspired tendons made from cables were used to control finger movements. Ad-
ditionally, Qin [ 16] demonstrated the feasibility of cable-driven actuation and the
effectiveness of its control methods through a continuum robot with redundant de-
grees of freedom and a posterior actuation mechanism, which is driven by steel wire
ropes. However, cable-driven systems have exhibited several critical limitations in
practical applications. When robots are required to perform contact operations with
the environment, the lack of suﬀicient compliance in cable-driven muscles renders them
ill-suited to adapt to the complex and variable contact forces. This significantly in-
creases the diﬀiculty of manipulation tasks. Moreover, the structural characteristics of
cable-driven systems pose challenges in achieving large-range, highly flexible bending
motions. As a result, it remains a significant challenge to replicate the natural and
fluid movements of human muscles.
In this paper, the bionic structural muscles driven by cables fall within the category
of soft actuators. The development of soft actuators has, to a certain extent, over-
come some shortcomings of traditional actuators. ElastoAmerican Origami, various
shapes of origami structures, and 3D printed soft actuators are developed using elastic

contact operations and large muscle applications [ 17]; Although HASEL actuators and

BMS aims to simulate the contraction mechanism of human muscles by combining
flexible and deformable 3D pattern structures with line driven mechanisms. Human
muscles produce contraction movements through myosin and actin filaments, while
small actuators maintain relative positions within the muscles to drive contractions.
This study converts this mechanism into line driven contraction. Traditional line
driven muscles are diﬀicult to achieve various shapes similar to human muscles due to
their lack of thickness. On the other hand, in order to enable BMS to better route
lines along muscle fiber pathways, create flexible structures in wireless areas, and
maintain muscle shape during contraction, thus achieving movements closer to human
muscles. BMS needs to have flexibility and range of motion in the x direction to achieve
contraction, suﬀicient strength in the y direction to support the structure and allow
contraction in the x direction, and a certain strength in the z direction to maintain the
positional relationship between the circuit and surrounding structures. For this, we
referred to the work of Yoshimura et al. [ 22], whose research on patterned structure
muscle effectively addressed this type of problem. However, they did not delve deeper
into the restoration of the degrees of freedom of the musculoskeletal system and its
motion control. We will apply these biomimetic muscles to human bone models based
on their foundation and complete the construction of their driving control system.

Methodology
3.1 Build the left upper limb 3D model
In the design of a highly biomimetic humanoid robotic arm driven by biomimetic
muscle structures, before designing the muscle model, it is necessary to first determine
the design scheme of the skeletal model. To give full play to the optimal performance
of the biomimetic muscles in this study, the designed skeletal model should accurately
restore the degrees of freedom of human bones as much as possible. For the human
upper arm, forearm, and elbow joint, as shown in Figure 3.1, they are abstracted and
simplified into a structure composed of two cylinders connected by hinges. This design
retains all the original degrees of freedom of human bones while effectively reducing
the modeling complexity.
Figure 3.1: Schematic diagram of elbow joint design
Additionally, as illustrated in Figure 3.2, the shoulder joint is designed as a spherical
joint. Since spherical joints can achieve multi - angle movements ranging from simple
to complex and can effectively cover various motion conditions, they can accurately

simulate the movement characteristics of the human shoulder joint with three degrees
of freedom, providing a reliable structural basis for the biomimetic movement of the
robotic arm [ 23].
Figure 3.2: Schematic diagram of shoulder joint design
After completing the design and modeling of the skeletal structure using SolidWorks
software, considering the maximization of the performance of the biomimetic muscles,
we carefully selected resin as the 3D printing material for the skeletal model. The
resin material has unique performance advantages. On the one hand, it can ensure
that the skeletal model maintains good hardness to meet the requirements of its struc-
tural strength. On the other hand, this material can achieve the lightweight of the
model, avoiding the negative impact on the performance of the biomimetic muscles
due to excessive weight. Thus, the biomimetic muscles can fully exert their optimal
performance during movement and achieve the expected biomimetic effect.
3.2 Design the Biomimetic Muscles Structure (BMS)
Before initiating the design of the muscle model, we first conducted a comprehensive
and in - depth analysis and reasonable simplification of the human muscles that drive
the movements of the shoulder and elbow joints.
Regarding the flexion and extension movements of the elbow joint, the main muscles
involved in generating force include the biceps brachii, triceps brachii, brachialis, and
the anterior forearm muscles. To facilitate subsequent modeling and research, based
on the specific roles of each muscle group in the flexion and extension movements of

===== PAGE 18 =====

the elbow joint, we carried out a reasonable classification and substitution process.
Specifically, the biceps brachii, brachialis, and the anterior forearm muscles were col-
lectively represented by Muscle 1, while the triceps brachii was independently modeled
as Muscle 2.
For the shoulder joint, the main muscles involved in shoulder activities are the deltoid
muscle (including the anterior, middle, and posterior bundles), teres major muscle,
pectoralis major muscle, latissimus dorsi muscle, and infraspinatus muscle. In order
to better reflect the functional characteristics of these muscles in shoulder movements,
we optimized them into Muscles 3−11 according to their functions.
Next, we will commence the simulation study of these two biomimetic muscle struc-
tures. In this research, we draw on the PSM structure proposed by Yoshimura et al.
[22] to improve our existing reticular muscle biomimetic structure, aiming to precisely
mimic the contraction mechanism of human muscles. The contraction of human mus-
cles is achieved through the interaction between actin and myosin filaments. These
microscopic actuators can maintain their relative positions and generate driving forces
during the contraction process. Our design concept is to transform this ingenious bio-
logical mechanism into an innovative muscle - inspired structure that can be effectively
applied in the field of musculoskeletal robots.
Specifically, we achieve the function of micro - actuators by carefully designing an X -
shaped lattice (Figure 3.3 (a) ) and causing it to undergo controlled deformation. The
constructed lattice muscle biomimetic structure combines the advantages of the X -
lattice elastic structure and TPU flexible material, endowing it with the ability to op-
erate flexibly under diverse shapes and complex environmental conditions. Traditional
wire - driven muscles typically achieve contraction by stretching the wires between the
muscle endpoints. However, due to the lack of thickness of the wires themselves, the
structural deformation can only occur along the direction of the wires, resulting in
a single form of deformation and making it diﬀicult to simulate the rich and diverse
shape changes similar to those of human muscle activities. To address this issue, we
constructed an X - shaped lattice structure in the wire - free area and embedded Z
- shaped (Figure 3.3 (b) ) blocks with rope channels in this lattice structure. This
design ingeniously balances the needs of maintaining the muscle shape and fixing and
deforming the ropes.

Furthermore, this structure needs to have anisotropy, which can exhibit different me-

in the X direction, so as to respond to the dynamic changes of working conditions
in a timely and effective manner; When external pressure sources exist, the grid-like
muscle structure in the Y direction needs to have suﬀicient strength and stability to
maintain the overall structural form and ensure the normal contraction function in
the X direction. The lattice muscle structure needs to remain stable in the Z direction
to ensure the stability of the relative position between the wire and the surrounding
structure. This stability ensures that during the structural deformation process, the
overall shape of the muscle is effectively maintained by ensuring that the route of the
wires is not disturbed. The basic structure of bionic muscles is shown in Figure 3.5.
To achieve the above goals, we must adjust the parameters x, y, z in Figure 3.3
(a). When the deformation ε of the lattice meets condition 0 ≤ ε ≤ 1 − 2p/x,
0 ≤ ε ≤ 1 − 2p/y, and 0 ≤ ε ≤ 1 − 2p/z, the main deformation of the lattice
is manifested as the deformation of the branches that constitute the lattice. In this
state, the lattice has a relatively large space for compression and extension. However,
when the deformation ε satisfies condition ε > 1 − 2p/x, ε > 1 − 2p/y, ε > 1 − 2p/z,
the lattice can no longer be compressed. It will become hard and play a supporting
role in this direction. Its degree of freedom and flexibility in expansion are greatly
limited, as shown in Figure 3.4. It can be seen that the values of the parameters x, y,
z are closely related to the characteristics of the lattice in all directions.

5.1 Conclusion
This study focuses on designing an artificial upper limb robotic arm inspired by the
muscle contraction mechanism of the human body and driven by the Biomimetic Mus-
cle Structure (BMS). During the research process, a three-dimensional model of the left
upper limb was successfully constructed, and the skeletal structure of this model accu-
rately restores the degrees of freedom of human bones. The designed BMS integrates
a cable-driven system, an X-lattice elastic structure, and TPU flexible materials. By
means of the X-lattice structure, the anisotropy and deformation behavior of biologi-
cal muscles are effectively simulated. In addition, the BMS also successfully replicates
the coordinated contraction of key muscle groups, such as the biceps brachii, triceps
brachii, and deltoid muscle.
In terms of the control system, the polynomial regression method is adopted. This
method can effectively fit the motion curves of muscles and joints, thereby accurately
establishing the relationship between muscle contraction and joint angles. The experi-
mental results show that the designed robotic arm can achieve motion patterns highly
similar to those of the human arm, and has relatively low values of Root Mean Squared
Error (RMSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) in motion curve fitting, which fully
verifies the accuracy of the control strategy employed.
However, it should not be overlooked that this study also has certain limitations. The
workspace analysis conducted through Monte Carlo simulation reveals a significant
difference between the reachable area of the robotic arm and that of the human arm.
Specifically, the relatively small volume of Muscle 7 in the shoulder region restricts the
abduction range of the shoulder joint. At the same time, the high-density resin material
used to construct the skeletal model increases the overall weight, further limiting the
ability of the robotic arm to perform full-range movements. These factors jointly

restrict the motion range and adaptability of the robotic arm in complex environments.
Despite the above limitations, this study still provides a new idea and method for the
design of biomimetic upper limb models. By demonstrating the feasibility of simulating
human muscle functions in robotic systems, this study has made positive contributions
to the development of the robotics field and the biomechanics field.
5.2 Future works
To further enhance the performance and applicability of the proposed model, subse-
quent research will focus on the following systematic explorations:
Firstly, refined modeling and optimization of human muscle groups are crucial. In -
depth improvements to the Biomimetic Muscle Structure (BMS) model, especially in
complex motion areas such as the shoulder, are expected to significantly enhance the
motion performance of the robotic arm. Specifically, by adjusting muscle structure
parameters and volume ratios, it is possible to effectively expand the muscle contrac-
tion stroke and increase the driving force output, thereby optimizing the motion range
and flexibility of the robotic arm.
Secondly, material innovation represents a key pathway for breaking through perfor-
mance bottlenecks. Future research will focus on exploring novel lightweight and high
- strength materials, such as carbon fiber composites and high - performance engi-
neering polymers, to replace the existing resin materials used for 3D - printed skeletal
models. This approach can ensure the mechanical integrity of the skeletal structure
while significantly reducing the overall weight, effectively improving the motion range
and energy conversion eﬀiciency of the robotic arm, optimizing its power - to - weight
ratio, and laying a foundation for more eﬀicient motion control.
Thirdly, the enhancement and upgrading of the control system are indispensable. Fu-
ture research plans to integrate advanced adaptive control algorithms, such as re-
inforcement learning algorithms and neural network models. These algorithms can
dynamically adjust muscle driving strategies by real - time environmental perception
and achieve coordinated control of multiple muscles (e.g., synchronous regulation of
antagonistic muscle groups). This will enable the robotic arm to achieve more natural
and smooth motion patterns and significantly improve its flexibility and adaptability
in complex environments.
Fourthly, the exploration and application of sensor integration technology will be an
important development direction. Force sensors, tactile sensors, and other sensors are
planned to be embedded in the BMS to construct a real - time feedback control loop,
thereby enhancing the interaction safety and operation accuracy in human - robot
