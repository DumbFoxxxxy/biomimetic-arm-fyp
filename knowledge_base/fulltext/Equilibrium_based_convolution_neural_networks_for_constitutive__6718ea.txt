ABSTRACT 
Recently, various machine learning methods have been developed to model the stress-strain 
constitutive behavior of materials. In these methods, a large database of multi-axial stress 
versus strain curves is indispensable for training and cross-validation. However, it is usually 
formidable to experimentally obtain such curves by noting that, although strain can be easily 
measured, stress can only be extracted for limited specimens with a simple and uniform stress 
field upon loading. Moreover, most developed approaches are based upon supervised learning 
and ignore known physical laws, resulting in limited interpretability and generalization. In this 
paper, an equilibrium-based convolution neural network (ECNN) is proposed to model the 
constitutive behavior of hyperelastic materials. Only strain and externally applied force are taken 
as input for training while the hard-to-measure stress is considered as internal variables. There-
fore, a large database can be easily generated with a single non-uniformly deformed specimen 
with spatially dependent strain. The equation of equilibrium is embedded in the architecture of 
the ECNN as a constraint to implicitly establish the correspondence between the internal variables 
and the stress components and, consequently, the constitutive stress–strain relationship. More-
over, the ECNN is unsupervised by noting that training is not against the output stress. Numerical 
and experimental examples are presented to show that the developed ECNN can faithfully extract 
the stress corresponding to local strain, model the multiaxial stress-strain constitutive behavior of 
hyperelastic materials, and surrogate equation-based conventional constitutive models of 
hyperelastic materials. 
1. Introduction 
Owing to recent advances in artificial intelligence and computing power, there is emerging interest in machine learning-based 
constitutive models of materials, e.g., data-driven computing methods to bypass constitutive modeling (Bessa et al., 2017; Iba˜nez 
et al., 2017; Kirchdoerfer and Ortiz, 2016), knowledge informed algorithms to extract analytical constitutive models from data 
(Champion et al., 2019; Flaschel et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2020; Lai and Nagarajaiah, 2019; Wang et al., 2022), among others. 
Although the latter are very attractive, they are conducive to systems with a simple representation that the space of possible functions 
is sparse. As a result, when there is no sufficient knowledge on the constitutive behavior of a material such as nematic elastomers 
(Conti et al., 2002) and mechanical metamaterials (Meng et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2021) or a simple analytical 
model does not even exist, their applications are limited.

Artificial neural networks (ANNs) have a strong nonlinear fitting capability and are a powerful means that can learn from data and 
make predictions (LeCun et al., 2015). Through automatic differentiation, they can calculate loss and back-propagate to efficiently 
update network parameters and can thus be easily applied to various practical problems (Baydin et al., 2018). Recently, ANNs have 
been employed to study various mechanics problems, including fracture mechanics (Liu et al., 2020; Wang and Chen, 2019), design of 
metamaterials (Bessa et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020), soft robot control (Han et al., 2018; Thuruthel et al., 2019), hydromechanics 
(Brunton et al., 2020; Raissi et al., 2019), biomechanics (Halilaj et al., 2018; McCoy et al., 2021), among others. As a type of ANNs, 
convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are particularly successful in the field of image recognition because of the advantages of local 
perception and parameter sharing (Szegedy et al., 2015). They also have applications in the field of mechanics, e.g., prediction of 
elastic constants of materials via training and validation through pixel processing of the images (Xue et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021; Ye 
et al., 2021). 
ANNs have shown their potential to surrogate the constitutive modeling and a number of studies have used ANNs as a black-box to 
establish the stress–strain constitutive relationship of materials (Frankel et al., 2019; Jenab et al., 2016; Lefik and Schrefler, 2003; 
Pandya et al., 2020). To account for the deformation history dependent feature of elastoplastic materials, neural networks with 
memory effects, e.g., recurrent neural networks - RNNs (Mozaffar et al. 2019) and gated recurrent units - a branch of RNNs (Gorji et al. 
2020), have been explored. Bonatti and Mohr (2022) discussed the self-consistency of the developed RNNs for modeling elastoplastic 
behavior with different incremental loading sizes. However, generalization and interpretability are two widely recognized issues 
inherent in ANN approaches. Linka et al. (2021) utilized invariant functions to construct the constitutive artificial neural networks of 
hyperelastic materials from given data in terms of stress and strain. Masi et al. (2021) have developed a thermodynamics-based 
constitutive model through a combination of several ANNs, with the thermodynamics laws embedded into the networks via con-
straints. Wen et al. (2021) established a physics-driven machine learning algorithm to map temperature, stress, and rate-dependent 
deformation in Li-metals. Other physics laws such as mass conservation, Navier-Stokes equation, and hysteretic constitutive re-
lations can also be considered in a systematic way of establishing neural networks with nonlinear analytic constraints (Bar-Sinai et al., 
2019; Raissi et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020), Beucler et al. (2021), bridging the gap between the disadvantages of ANNs that are hardly 
interpretable and abundant physical models developed by academic alike over years. ANNs with these flexible constructions can be 
particularly advantageous and attractive for materials that are too complex to be readily modeled by simple analytical algebra, dif-
ferential, and/or integral equations and vast pre-knowledge is available. 
In machine learning-based constitutive models of materials, a large database of multi-axial stress–strain curves is required. 
However, it is usually expensive and formidable to experimentally obtain multiaxial stress–strain curves, by noting that stress can only 
be directly inferred from special specimens under specific loadings. Alternatively, numerical tests such as finite element simulations 
can be resorted to. In fact, almost all the aforementioned machining learning models are based on numerically generated data. 
However, numerical tests are not applicable when the constitutive behaviors of the constituent components and their interactions of 
the material to be considered are not prior known, which is especially true for emerging new materials. 
In this paper, an equilibrium-based convolution neural network (ECNN) for the constitutive modeling of hyperelastic materials is 
proposed. Only readily measurable quantities (i.e., displacement, strain, and externally applied force) are taken as input data for 
training while stress is treated as internal variables. Using a single specimen with non-uniform deformation upon loading, massive 
training data can be obtained, greatly facilitating data generation. By enforcing the equation of equilibrium as an inherent constraint in 
the ECNN, stress corresponding to a given set of strain can be obtained. Thus, the trained ECNN can serve as a constitutive model. The 
paper is organized as follows. Section 2 shows the framework of constitutive modeling of hyperelastic materials with the ECNN. The 
structure of the ECNN and implementation of the constraint in the form of the equilibrium equation are given in Section 3. Numerical 
and experimental examples are presented in Sections 4 and 5, respectively. Finally, a few concluding remarks are presented. 
2. Framework of ECNN-based constitutive modeling 
Machine learning-based constitutive modeling of materials requires a large database of multi-axial stress–strain curves for training 
and cross-validation, a network algorithm with parameters trained from the database, and model prediction, amongst others. In 
general, the stress–strain curves should cover the strain and strain spaces as wide as possible to provide reliable training. Therefore, 
numerous tests with different loading conditions must be conducted. 
In experiments, although strain can be relatively easy to measure, e.g., through the digital image correlation method, or DIC (Chu 
et al., 1985; Pan et al., 2009), stress is only directly measurable for specimens when the stress field inside the specimen gauge region is 
uniform and can be directly inferred from the externally applied loading. Indeed, such specimens are limited to particular geometries, 
e.g., dog-bone type plates or cylinder specimens for uniaxial loading, cruciform specimens for biaxial loading, thin-walled tubular 
specimens for combined axial and torsional loading, cylinder specimens for triaxial loading (ASTM, 2015). Generally, one set of 
uniaxial or multiaxial stress–strain curves can be obtained for each test. To obtain another set of curves, one has to conduct a different 
loading test with a new specimen (or the same specimen if the deformation is reversible). There is no surprise that multiple tests are 
very costly, especially for multi-axial testing. 
Alternatively, one can perform numerical tests to generate multiaxial stress–strain curves through various micromechanics 
modeling techniques (Nemat-Nasser and Hori, 2013). The biggest limitation of numerical tests lies in that the constituent components 
and their interactions must follow either a known or pre-assumed constitutive behavior, in addition to the issues of accuracy and 
time-consuming of the currently available micromechanics methods. Therefore, when the constitutive behavior of the constituent 
components of materials (especially for emerging new materials) is not prior known, numerical tests are deemed to be not feasible to 
construct a database of stress–strain curves for machine learning-based constitutive modeling.

In this study, the framework of the proposed equilibrium-based convolution neural network (ECNN) for the constitutive modeling 
of hyperelastic materials comprises two parts, i.e., (i) a scheme to generate a large database of multiaxial stress–strain curves from a 
single specimen and (ii) a neural network to identify a constitutive model via training, as illustrated in Fig. 1. In the following, only 
two-dimensional (2D) constitutive modeling is considered. Extension of the developed framework to three dimensional (3D) problems 
will be discussed in the last section of the paper.

The key feature of the specimen is that it deforms non-uniformly upon loading. Compared to uniformly deformed specimens that can 
only provide one set of stress–strain responses for each test, non-uniformly deformed specimens can generate a database comprising a 
large number of stress–strain curves from different material points with a single test. By increasing the spatial resolution, the 
magnitude of loading, and the number of loading paths, the database can be further expanded. 
As pointed out earlier, the challenge is how to extract the spatial stress components in non-uniform deformation. To this end, an 
unsupervised convolution neural network is developed as shown in Fig. 1b. The measured strain at each material point and the 
corresponding external force at each step constitute a set of input data, with the corresponding stress components treated as unknown 
internal variables and served as output. These internal variables must satisfy the equation of equilibrium, regardless of whether the 
material is elastic or not. Therefore, we enforce the equilibrium equation as an inherent constraint of the convolution neural network, 
which is thus equilibrium-based. Once the ECNN is trained, the internal variables are obtained, i.e., the relationship between stress and 
strain, i.e., the stress-strain constitutive behavior, is determined. Details of the ECNN are given in Section 3. 
With the generated large database of stress–strain curves, one can use various machine learning methods (Flaschel et al., 2021; 
Wang et al., 2022) to extract the underlying mathematical formulae-based models with frame-indifferent variables (e.g., principal 
stretches and invariants). In real hyperelastic materials, however, it is not trivial to derive analytical expressions. In some cases, a 
simple expression may not exist at all. Therefore, we don’t derive analytical constitutive models. Instead, it is more efficient to 
establish a constitutive relationship through the ECNN by learning directly from experimental data. 
3. Equilibrium-based convolution neural network (ECNN) 
3.1. Structure of ECNN 
The proposed ECNN is composed of a convolutional neural network with one fully connected layer, as shown in Fig. 2a for N sets of 
data in T time steps. Discussion of N and T will be given in Section 3.2. For the considered 2D plane strain problem, three in-plane strain 
components (i.e., ε11, ε22 and ε12) of each set are taken as the input channels. 
In the calculation, the strains of different sets are independent and, for each set of strain, a set of three internal variables repre-
senting the corresponding stress components is introduced and is yet to be evaluated. A 1× 1 convolution kernel is adopted to avoid 
coupling between different sets of strain inputs. The internal variables for each strain set are calculated independently. All strain 
components are normalized to improve the computation stability. The neural network used in this paper consists of two parts, as 
illustrated in Fig. 2a and 2b, respectively. First, the data of three input strain channels is expanded to higher dimensional channels by 
two-step convolutions (N1× N2, with N1= 60 and N2= 180 shown in Fig. 2a). It is noted that the stress components may differ in 
orders of magnitude. For instance, under pure shear loading, shear stress can be much larger than normal stress. Under such cir-
cumstances, if we output the internal variables through fully connected layers (i.e., the linear operation), inaccurate results may occur. 
Therefore, a second part - a group convolution is introduced to split the channels into three sub-groups, each having N2 /3 channels at 
the beginning and gradually decreasing to 1 by three layers (N3× N4× 1, with N3= 40 and N4= 20 shown in Fig. 2b) and N sets of 
internal variables. In total, there are T× 3N internal variables following the group convolution where N represents the number of 
elements connected to the input node and T denotes the time steps in loading. A detailed discussion on the ECNN network and the 
selection of hyperparameters can be found in Appendix A. It should be emphasized that stress does not participate in training. In 
general, more layers and channels can be adopted to improve the fitting capability of the ECNN, at the expense of training cost. 
3.2. Equilibrium constraint and loss functions

the internal variables to have the physical meaning of stress components, we output the nodal force through a fully connected layer 
that is based on the equilibrium condition and minimization of loss functions at the nodal level. By doing so, the weight of the layer can 
be obtained. Details are given as follows.

4. Numerical examples 
4.1. Data generation 
A 2D finite element (FE) model of a biaxially loaded square plate with a central hole (in Fig. 3a) is employed for the purpose of 
numerical demonstration of the developed ECNN. The FE computed strain field mimics experimental measurement, e.g., DIC. The 
dimensions of the plate and hole are H and R, respectively. The plate is subject to displacement controlled biaxial loading denoted by u1 
and u2, with the corresponding reaction forces being F1 and F2, respectively. The deliberately introduced hole renders the deformation

compared to uniformly deformed specimens. It should be pointed out that there are additional means to introduce non-uniform

4.2. Training of ECNN 
Note that stress is output instead of input quantity of the ECNN and is treated as internal variables satisfying the constraint of the 
equilibrium equation. Training is conducted in virtue of strain and externally applied forces. The ECNN is thus unsupervised. Unlike 
supervised learning, there is no need for the ECNN to divide the data into training and cross-validation sets, and all data is used as 
training. First, the effect of the mapping and group convolution parameters in the ECNN on training error is exploited. It is found that 
the training error decreases with an increasing number of parameters (results are shown in Appendix A). In particular, the set of 
parameters with N1= 60, N2= 180, N3= 40, and N4= 20 is found to be adequate to provide good accuracy while retain computational 
cost. For a balanced accuracy and cost, this set of parameters is adopted in all the following calculations. With the data obtained from 
the FE model, we repeat the training process 100 times under the same condition to eliminate the influence of random initialization.

5. Experimental examples 
To further validate the ECNN, experiment examples are presented. Two specimens made of hyperelastic soft rubber material are 
manufactured. Both specimens have in-plane dimensions of 40 mm×40 mm and an out-of-plane thickness of 40 mm. This particular 
thickness is chosen so that the plane strain assumption can be approximately satisfied. The first specimen has an elliptical hole with R1 
= 8 mm and R2= 4 mm (Fig. 11a) and the second one has a central circular hole with R= 6 mm (Fig. 12a). Displacement controlled 
uniaxial compressive tests are conducted with an IBTC-300 test machine. The imposed maximum displacement is uy = 2 mm within 
about 100 steps. The loading rate is set to 0.02 mm/s, corresponding to 5× 10  4/s in strain rate to mimic quasi-static loading. 
Before testing, grey speckles are sprayed to the specimen surfaces to facilitate post-processing. Images of the deformed speckle 
patterns at various loading steps are recorded using a high-resolution digital camera (SONY FDR-AX40, Tokyo, Japan). The defor-
mation information is extracted via the DIC method through the commercial software XTDIC (XTOP 3D Technology, Shenzhen, China). 
Once the coordinate, displacement, and strain field are obtained, they are projected to the corresponding meshes with triangular 
elements. Then, we use the experimental data for the developed ECNN, following the same procedure as that for the numerical ex-
amples in Section 4. It is noted that, under uniaxial loading, the deformation information of the specimen with an elliptical hole is more 
diverse than that of the second specimen with a circular central hole. Therefore, data generated from the first specimen is used as 
training while that from the second specimen serves as testing. Because 100 steps generate too much data, data from only 8 steps of 
loading is used in training. 
The DIC measured strain contours at the last loading step of the first specimen are given in Fig. 11b, showing the non-uniform strain 
distribution. Because the true stress of the specimen is not known, we are not able to assess the predicted stress directly. Nevertheless, 
the applied forces can be computed from the ECNN predicted stress field and are compared with the external forces imposed by the test 
machine (referred to as true forces). Results are summarized in Fig. 11c for all loading levels, showing excellent agreement between 
each other. With the developed ECNN, stress contours corresponding to the measured strain can be predicted and are plotted in 
Fig. 11d. 
Moreover, the trained ECNN can be employed to predict the stress of the second specimen upon compression (Fig. 12a), corre-
sponding to the measured strain field. A set of typical DIC measured strain contours is presented in Fig. 12b, at the externally applied 
compressive force of 113N in magnitude. From the predicted stress, the externally applied forces at various loading levels can be 
obtained and are compared with those read directly from the test machine in Fig. 12c, showing good agreement. Corresponding stress 
components are output by ECNN in Fig. 12d. The experimental results shown above provide added confidence in the reliability of the 
developed ECNN as the role of constitutive modeling. 
6. Discussion and concluding remarks 
In general, knowledge of the stress–strain response of a material is indispensable for the development of its constitutive model. 
However, without a constitutive law (which is always true for new materials), stress can only be directly inferred from the externally 
applied loading for limited specimens with a specific geometry, compared to strain that can be relatively easy to measure. The dilemma 
is further exaggerated for machine learning-based constitutive modeling in which a large database of multiaxial stress-strain responses 
is required. The proposed constitutive modeling framework for hyperelastic materials with an equilibrium-based convolution neural 
network (ECNN) has, to a large extent, addressed the bottleneck. Numerical and experimental examples are presented to demonstrate 
that, for a given local strain the ECNN is capable of extracting the corresponding stress components. Signal noise is shown to only have 
a mild effect on the modeling capacity of the ECNN. Moreover, it is found that the trained ECNN can faithfully serve the role of 
constitutive modeling and can thus surrogate equation-based conventional constitutive models of hyperelastic materials. 
In the framework, a specimen with non-uniform deformation upon loading is employed to generate data for neural network 
training. It is advantageous in at least three aspects. First, only readily obtainable quantities (e.g., displacement, strain, and external 
loading) are required in the training set of data while the hard-to-measure stress is treated as internal variables embedded in the ECNN. 
By enforcing a constraint in the weak form of the equilibrium equation into the ECNN, stress can be extracted. Second, once the stress is 
identified, using the spatial dependent and auxiliary multiaxial feature of non-uniform deformation, a single specimen test can 
generate numerous multiaxial work conjugates in terms of stress and strain at various material points, and thus a large database of 
multiaxial stress-strain responses. Therefore, costly multiaxial tests can be reduced or even avoided. The database can be further 
expanded by varying loading steps and loading paths. Third, the ECNN is unsupervised because stress is output instead of an input 
variable of training, which greatly reduces the complexity of training and cross-validation of a neural network. Moreover, the ECNN is 
physics (equilibrium) informed, providing increased interpretability and generalization of the machine learning-based constitutive 
model. 
It should be pointed out that although only 2D examples are presented for the purpose of demonstration, the proposed framework is 
not limited to 2D problems. When 3D strain measurement technologies, e.g., the volumetric digital image correlation, or V-DIC 
(Luetkemeyer et al., 2021) can be used to measure the deformation inside bodies, an extension of the framework to 3D problems is 
straightforward. However, we do realize that only history independent hyperelastic constitutive behaviors are considered in the ECNN. 
Extension of the ECNN to model history dependent behavior (e.g., elastoplasticity) is highly desirable. One possibility is to use in-
cremental strain and stress instead of total ones and replace the convolution neural network with other machine learning algorithms 
with a memory effect, e.g., recurrent neural networks (Bonatti and Mohr, 2022; Gorji et al., 2020; Mozaffar et al., 2019), which is 
currently under investigation.

The workflow of the ECNN is summarized in Fig. A1. A node can be associated with multiple elements. To be specific, consider two 
cases. In case 1, node i is connected to three elements. We take the three strain components of the surrounding three elements as input, 
with dimensions of T× 3× 3× 1. After convolution, the ECNN outputs the internal variables as Cauchy stress components of di-
mensions being T× 9. We convert the Cauchy stress to the first P-K stress with dimensions of T× 12. Following Eq. (2), we can 
calculate the external nodal force by the ECNN and get the loss in training. No activation function and bias are required. For different 
nodes, the dimension of the fully connected layer is related to the number of elements connected by the node. In case 2, node j is 
connected to four elements. The dimension of the output of convolutional layers is T× 3× 4× 1. We convert the Cauchy stress to the 
first P-K stress as T× 16. Following Eq. (2), we can generate a weight matrix of dimension T× 16. The external force dimension of the 
final output is T× 2. It is straightforward to extend the procedure to other cases, such as nodes connected with N elements where the 
weight dimension of the fully connected layer is 4N× 2. For nodes on the boundary, we obtain the strain components of all elements 
connected to the nodes and calculate the total of the external nodal forces and loss. We sum the loss of internal and boundary nodes and 
update parameters by backpropagation as one epoch. After training, we remove the fully connected layer and use the internal variables 
as output (i.e., predicted stress components). 
In the convolution, we use a 1×1 convolution kernel, equivalent to a multilayer perceptron. We use the CNN structure for the strain- 
to-stress process with the help of the concept of channels.
