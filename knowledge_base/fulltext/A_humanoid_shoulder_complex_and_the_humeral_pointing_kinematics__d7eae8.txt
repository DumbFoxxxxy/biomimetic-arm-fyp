A Humanoid Shoulder Complex and
the Humeral Pointing Kinematics
Jadran Lenarˇciˇc and Michael Staniˇsic´
Abstract—This paper presents a humanoid robotic shoulder complex
and the kinematics of humanoid humeral pointing as performed by this
complex. The humanoid shoulder complex is composed of two subsystems,
a parallel mechanism which serves as the innermost shoulder girdle and
a serial mechanism which serves as the outermost spherical glenohumeral
joint. These two subsystems are separated by an offset distance and a twist
angle. The subsystems operate cooperatively as an offset double pointing
system. Humanoid humeral pointing is defined as a configuration in which
the displacement of the shoulder girdle and the humerus are coplanar, and
in which a ratio between an inclination angle in each subsystem achieves a
constant value consistent with human humeral pointing. One redundant de-
gree of freedom remains in the humanoid shoulder girdle, and it can be used
to optimize system configuration and operating criteria, such as avoiding
the singular cones of the humanoid glenohumeral joint.
Index Terms—Humanoid robot, humanoid shoulder, kinematics, parallel
mechanism, shoulder girdle.
I. I NTRODUCTION
One recent target in industrial and service robotics is the develop-
ment of a humanoid manipulator. By learning from solutions in nature,
robot designers are developing new manipulator designs and control
strategies in order to reproduce human-like motion and to achieve the
desired human-like mobility, versatility, redundancy, or compliance.
Humans in the future may be expected to work in the vicinity of
and alongside robots. Eventually, humans will teach, supervise, and
cooperatively work with robots. To reach this level of man-machine
synergy, the human-to-robot interface must be simplified/improved.
The interest in humanoid robotics is to simplify this interface. For
example, if the motion and structure of a robot were similar to that of
a human (i.e., a humanoid robot):
• a human coworker would better anticipate the machine’s move-
ments;
• the robot would have a human level of dexterity (unlike today’s
industrial robots);
• the robot would more easily learn its motion by observation of a
kinematically similar human.
Mechanically, a humanoid can be thought of as having the following
subsystems:
• a multifingered dexterous hand for grasping and fine manipula-
tions [1];
• a wrist for controlling hand orientation [2]–[4];
• an arm (shoulder complex, upper arm, elbow, and forearm) for
controlling hand position [5], [6];
• a waist for attitude control of the torso [7];
• biped locomotion (hip, upper leg, knee, lower leg, ankle, and foot)
for gross movements [8].

With the exception of the humanoid shoulder complex, these me-
chanical subsystems have been relatively well studied and several pro-
totype subsystems have been developed. According to [9], “So little
has been done with robot shoulders that this is a virgin area for de-
signers.” The “shoulder” of industrial manipulators usually contains
either a universal or a spherical joint. However, the human shoulder
complex is more than just a simple ball-and-socket joint. In [10] a five
degree-of-freedom (DOF) humanoid shoulder was proposed. A precise
replica of the human shoulder based on the use of McKibben actuators
was reported in [11]. By building robotic replicas of biological systems,
there is a hope to engineer better robots as well as learn more about bi-
ology [12].
The human shoulder is an extremely sophisticated and interrelated
system that can produce an infinity of complex spatial motions.
The goal of this work is to present the kinematics of the humanoid
humeral pointing, a movement which is understood as the primary
task of the shoulder. Here, the shoulder complex is modeled as a
redundant system composed of two pointing subsystems separated
by an offset distance and a twist angle. These subsystems are the
shoulder girdle and the spherical glenohumeral joint, and they operate
cooperatively as an offset double-pointing system. Among an infinite
number of configurations of the humanoid shoulder that correspond to
a given humeral pointing direction, we define the humanoid humeral
pointing as a configuration in which the displacement of the shoulder
girdle and the humerus are coplanar and in which a ratio between
an inclination angle in each subsystem achieves a value consistent
with human humeral pointing. The offset distance between the two
pointing systems is changeable. In this paper, in order to mimic the
human proportions, it is treated as a dependent coordinate whose value
depends on the girdle’s inclination angle. The redundant twist between
the shoulder girdle and the glenohumeral joint can be used in the
control stage of the humanoid shoulder complex to avoid singularities
in the parallel girdle mechanism and to avoid the singular cones of the
glenohumeral joint.
This paper begins with a description of the principles that govern
the human shoulder motion, then a development of a humanoid robotic
shoulder complex is presented and the kinematics of the humeral
pointing are developed. In the end, we discuss some design and control
aspects associated with the proposed humanoid shoulder complex.
II. P RIMARY FUNCTION OF HUMAN SHOULDER
COMPLEX IN HUMAN ARM MOTIONS
Basic human shoulder motion capabilities and analytical explana-
tions of human arm proportions were reported in [13]–[16] . The human
shoulder complex is properly divided into two interconnected subsys-
tems. First is the innermost portion of the shoulder complex, referred
to as the shoulder girdle [17]. It consists of the sternum/thorax/torso,
clavicle, and scapula as seen in Fig. 1.
Second is the outermost portion of the shoulder complex, thegleno-
humeral joint [17], see Fig. 1. The glenohumeral joint moves with the
scapula of the shoulder girdle. The humerus connects to the scapula
through this glenohumeral joint.
In order to design an effective humanoid shoulder complex, it is nec-
essary to understand the primary function of the human shoulder com-
plex, which is to point (i.e., aim) the humerus. With addition of a roll
about the pointed humeral axis, the complete orientation of the elbow
is controlled. As reported by [14] and [15], motion within the human
shoulder girdle is primarily a consequence of this humeral pointing ac-
tion. That is to say, when the humerus is held in a fixed pointing direc-
tion and then a humeral roll is developed (in order to properly orient
the elbow), there is little to no additional movement observed within

A secondary function of the human shoulder complex is for “shrug-
ging.” In shrugging, the humeral axis is translated (i.e. no change in its
direction). Shrugging is a self-motion of the highly redundant human
shoulder complex. This self-motion may be used to optimize secondary
tasks which are not considered here. As well, shrugging may be used
in physical human communication, such as signaling, “I don’t know.”
This secondary function is not considered in this paper.
It is interesting to note that in the human shoulder complex in Fig. 1,
the shoulder girdle is a single closed-loop chain (the simplest type of
parallel mechanism), whereas the glenohumeral joint is a serial chain.
As the innermost portion of the shoulder complex, the shoulder girdle
bears the full load and inertial forces of the arm and manipulated object.
Thus, the shoulder girdle must have a high load-carrying capacity. At
the same time, motion developed within the shoulder girdle is signif-
icantly smaller than that within the glenohumeral joint. The shoulder
girdle motion just sufficiently supplements the glenohumeral joint mo-
tion so the human has nearly a hemisphere of humeral pointing ca-
pability [18]. Without the comparatively small motion contributions
of the shoulder girdle, the human’s humeral workspace would be sig-
nificantly less than this hemisphere. The shoulder girdle also allows
the scapula to move toward the front of the torso so that a human
can point the humerus across the front of the torso, and thus, reach
across the front of the torso without interfering with it. This “reaching
across the chest” motion would be severely limited without an articu-
lated shoulder girdle. Although the motion (articulations) within the
shoulder girdle are comparatively small, the girdle’s role is crucial
to achieving the large interference-free workspace that the human’s
humerus possesses. So, it is not surprising that the human shoulder
girdle is a simple single-loop parallel mechanism, since it is well known
in robotics that high load-carrying capacity with small motion capabil-
ities are exactly the properties of a parallel mechanism, in contrast to
serial mechanisms whose comparative properties are larger workspace
and lesser load-carrying capacity [19].

A quantitative description of the human shoulder girdle motion is
far from simple [17]. The movement patterns of single bones in the
girdle differ among individuals and may be influenced by a conscious
control, learning, and training. In Fig. 3, the shoulder girdle (as shown
in Fig. 2) is substituted by a simplified kinematic equivalent which
consists of a universal joint containing two rotations intersecting in
point /65 . The center of the glenohumeral joint is denoted by point /66 .
Point /83 , on the other hand, represents a fixed reference, and is placed
at the intersection of two anatomical horizontal axes (shown as dashed
lines), the posterior/anterior axis that passes through the center of the
sternoclavicular joint, and the medial/lateral axis that lies in the middle
of the torso. In order to mimic the distance between points/83 and /66 ,a n
additional translation must be introduced. It is a dependent coordinate
and can be treated as a function of the rotation angles in point /65 .
In [20], the relative position between points /83 and /66 was measured
in a number of male and female subjects performing different manip-
ulation tasks. It was observed that the maximum working cone of axis
/83/66 can be produced when the humerus is stretched downward. An av-
erage maximum cone angle is about 50/14. The central axis of this cone is
inclined typically about 10/14 upward and 5/14 forward, with respect to the
medial/lateral axis. During the shoulder motion, the distance between
points /83 and /66 changes as a function of the inclination angle between
axis /83/66 and the central axis of its working cone. The change is about
/6 10% of the average value. Typically, /83/66 shortens when the girdle
moves upward and backward and expands when the girdle is moving
downward and forward.
To obtain a desired relative position of point /66 with respect to
point /83 , the rotation center /65 can be chosen arbitrarily. However, by
changing the location of /65 , it is possible to regulate the amount of
translation in the direction of /83/65 and the amount of rotations in point
/65 . The location of point /65 is, therefore, important in the design of
the humanoid shoulder girdle. A design limitation that must be taken
into account are the ranges of rotations in point /65 . A commercial
universal joint has /54/52/14 /40/6 /51/50/14 /41 range. To fulfill this limit, point /65
must be fixed at a distance /65/66 , which is about 80% of the average
distance /83/66 . With this assumption, the overlapping of the working
cone of /65/66 with the working cone of /83/66 is achieved when axis /83/65
is inclined about 7/14 upward and forward with respect to the anatomical
medial/lateral anatomical axis. As a result of this placement of point
/65 , the translation in direction /83/65 will vary for about /6 8% of the
average distance /83/66 , which is also a reasonable design assumption.
During the elevation of the humerus, the glenoid fossa, on which the

Because of the kinematic redundancy of the human shoulder complex,
the pointing of the humeral axis can be produced in an immense number
of ways. In this paper, the humanoid pointing is defined as a unique
configuration in which the displacement of the shoulder girdle and the
humerus are coplanar, and in which the relationship between angles
/13/49 and /13 achieves a value consistent with a measured human ratio.
The described shoulder rhythm movement can, thus, be understood as
a specific case of the humanoid pointing.
III. D EVELOPMENT OF A HUMANOID SHOULDER COMPLEX
Prior humanoid shoulder complex designs have either considered
only the function of the human glenohumeral joint [6], [9], [24] or only
the function of the human shoulder girdle [25]–[28]. The humanoid
shoulder complex proposed in [6], [9] uses a symmetrically actuated
double-pointing system (developed earlier as a dextrous wrist [29],
[30]). The design lacks the shoulder girdle, but would serve well as
a glenohumeral joint whose range of motion must exceed the 64/14 cone
typically available with a single universal joint. The complex devel-
oped in [24] considers only a single universal joint as a glenohumeral
joint, also not incorporating a shoulder girdle. The primary goal there
is the actuation of the system through pneumatic McKibbon muscles.
The range of motion in this system will be less than the human’s, and
it will suffer from humerus to torso interferences when reaching across
the chest, due to the lack of an articulated shoulder girdle.
The research of [25], [26] considers the function of only the
humanoid shoulder girdle. They designed and built a cybernetic
shoulder girdle capable of human-like sensitivity compliance. Their
design offers compactness, large workspace, human-like motion,
singularity-free motion, and small backlash. A parallel mechanism
with four DOFs, which is capable of closely reproducing human
girdle motions, is described in [27] and [28]. No consideration of the
glenohumeral joint or the coupling of its motion to that within the
shoulder girdle is given.
In this paper, we consider the synthesis and kinematic control of
both subsystems (a cybernetic shoulder girdle and glenohumeral joint).
The kinematic control equations presented here incorporate a necessary
coupling between the motion of these two subsystems which has been
observed in human motion studies [14], [15].
In [17], a kinematic model is given for the human shoulder complex.
This is closely related to the model shown in Figs. 2 and in 3, which are
our starting point. Construction of the model in Fig. 3 as a humanoid
shoulder complex is a possibility, but the location and types of actuators
would be problematic.

Fig. 4 shows a serial humanoid shoulder complex which is capable of

/108/48 and /18/54 ) to develop the kinematic humeral pointing equations for the
humanoid shoulder complex.

VI. C ONCLUSIONS
We have demonstrated that the humanoid humeral pointing operation
can be performed by a series of two pointing systems /18/49 , /18/50 and /18/52 ,
/18/53 , separated by an offset distance /40/100/51 /41 and a twist angle /40/18/51 /41. The
first pointing system is included in the shoulder girdle and the second
in the spherical glenohumeral joint. Even though the shoulder girdle
possesses relatively small ranges of motion, it considerably enlarges
the humeral workspace by introducing the ability to avoid collisions
between the humerus and the torso, and by avoiding singularity cones
in the glenohumeral joint.
In this paper, we defined the humanoid humeral pointing as a
coplanar inclination in the shoulder girdle and the glenohumeral joint.
These two displacements are characterized by a coupling ratio between
the inclination angles that is consistent with the anatomy of a specific
human subject. We presented the direct and the inverse kinematics of
this motion for a humanoid robotic shoulder complex, which possesses
a shoulder girdle realized as a parallel platform mechanism and a serial
spherical glenohumeral joint attached to the girdle’s platform. With
respect to the task of pointing, this system is kinematically redundant
and can, therefore, produce an infinite number of configurations
that correspond to the humanoid humeral pointing. The kinematic
redundancy enables us to optimize the twist angle /18/51 with respect to
selected operating criteria. The results obtained in this paper are useful
in the design and control of a humanoid robotic shoulder.
